Temporal Difference Learning: the idea that lets an agent learn while it's still driving.

Session 9 fuses the two ideas from earlier in the course — Dynamic Programming's bootstrapping and Monte Carlo's model-free sampling — into TD(0), then builds SARSA and Q-Learning on top of it. This guide walks the lecture slide-by-slide, then re-derives every algorithm on a single running example: a race car managing its engine temperature, worked by hand from MDP through Double Q-Learning.

01DP vs MC → TD Fusion

Solving MDPs so far — and why TD had to exist

Before TD, the course covered two families of solution methods, and each had a fatal flaw the other one happened to fix.

Dynamic Programming

  • ✓ Off-policy
  • ✓ Local learning — bootstraps from neighbouring estimates
  • ✗ Model-based — needs full transition probabilities

Monte Carlo

  • ✗ On-policy (importance sampling can relax this)
  • ✗ Requires a full episode before it can update anything
  • ✓ Model-free, learns online from raw experience

The pirate-and-robot grid recap: four states w, x, y, z, with w paying −100 and z paying +10, γ = 0.9. Once a full episode {x, y, z, exit} is observed, Bellman optimality lets you discount the return backward from the goal:

Q(z, exit) = 10  →  Q(y, →) = 9  →  Q(x, →) = 8.1

General form — Bellman optimality: q*(s,a) = Σs′ p(s′|s,a)[ r(s,a,s′) + γ·maxa′ q*(s′,a′) ]

Fusing DP and MC

The "aha" moment of the lecture: what if you kept DP's trick of updating an estimate from a neighbouring estimate (bootstrapping), but dropped the requirement of knowing the environment's model — learning instead from raw, sampled experience the way MC does? That fusion is TD Learning.

TD Learning keeps DP's bootstrapping and MC's model-free, online learning — while dropping both of their weaknesses.
🔎 Deep Dive — why this matters historically

TD learning is considered one of the most original ideas in reinforcement learning, and it shows up in neuroscience too: dopamine neurons in the brain's ventral tegmental area and substantia nigra fire in a pattern that closely resembles the TD error signal — more when an outcome is better than expected, less when it's worse.

TD-Gammon: Gerald Tesauro's backgammon program trained with TD(λ) reached a level competitive with human grandmasters purely by playing itself and updating value estimates — no game-tree search, no hand-coded strategy. It was the first landmark proof that bootstrapped, model-free, online learning actually works in practice.

02TD(0) Prediction

Learning from a guess about a guess

In Monte Carlo, the return Gt is the actual discounted reward collected over a whole episode. TD(0)'s shortcut: replace Gt with an estimated return, Rt+1 + γV(St+1), and update toward that instead.

TD(0) update rule V(St) ← V(St) + α [ Rt+1 + γV(St+1) − V(St) ]
  • Rt+1 + γV(St+1) is the TD target — the "estimated return".
  • Rt+1 + γV(St+1) − V(St) is the TD error — how wrong the old estimate was.
  • MC updates toward the true return Gt; TD updates toward a bootstrapped estimate derived from the Bellman equation.
Tabular TD(0) for estimating vπ
Input: the policy π to be evaluated
Algorithm parameter: step size α ∈ (0, 1]
Initialize V(s), for all s ∈ S+, arbitrarily except V(terminal) = 0

Loop for each episode:
    Initialize S
    Loop for each step of episode:
        A ← action given by π for S
        Take action A, observe R, S'
        V(S) ← V(S) + α[R + γV(S') − V(S)]
        S ← S'
    until S is terminal

Every single step — not just at episode's end — you act, observe the reward and next state, and immediately nudge V(S) using the reward plus your current guess about the next state. Repeat until terminal.

🔎 Deep Dive — a relatable analogy

Driving to a new city with GPS: Monte Carlo refuses to update its mental time-estimate for any leg of the trip until you actually arrive — only then does it say "okay, that first highway stretch took 40 minutes." TD updates its estimate for each stretch as you drive, using the current best guess of what's still ahead. Hit unexpected traffic? You don't wait until the destination to react — you update immediately, combining the new information with your existing guess about what's left.

This immediacy is why TD tends to learn faster than MC in practice, especially on long or continuing tasks — and why it can be used on tasks that never formally end, where MC simply doesn't apply.

03On-Policy TD Control

SARSA — learning Q(s,a) from the action you actually took next

SARSA moves from state-values to action-values, so the agent can learn which action is best in a state, not just how good the state is. Its name is its five ingredients: State, Action, Reward, next State, next Action.

SARSA update rule Q(St,At) ← Q(St,At) + α [ Rt+1 + γQ(St+1,At+1) − Q(St,At) ]

Applied after every transition from a nonterminal state. If St+1 is terminal, Q(St+1,At+1) is defined as zero. It's called on-policy because A′ is chosen by the same ε-greedy policy the agent is actually following — SARSA learns the value of the policy it uses, warts and all.

SARSA (on-policy TD control) for estimating Q ≈ q*
Initialize Q(s,a) arbitrarily, Q(terminal-state, ·) = 0
Repeat (for each episode):
    Initialize S
    Choose A from S using policy derived from Q (e.g., ε-greedy)
    Repeat (for each step of episode):
        Take action A, observe R, S'
        Choose A' from S' using policy derived from Q (e.g., ε-greedy)
        Q(S,A) ← Q(S,A) + α[R + γQ(S',A') − Q(S,A)]
        S ← S'; A ← A'
    until S is terminal

Worked walkthrough — the pirate/robot grid

Grid: w (start, pirate) → x (robot) → y → z. Landing on w pays −100; reaching z pays +10. Q-table starts at all zeros; the deck animates this build one line of pseudocode at a time — condensed here to the moments where numbers actually change:

Step 1
Robot moves into w (the pirate's cell), receiving −100. Q(w) updates immediately to −100 — SARSA reacting to a bad outcome the instant it happens. Q-table: w = −100, x=(0,0), y=(0,0), z=0
Step 2
Bootstrapping shows up: because moving from x toward w leads to −100, the Q-value for that action from x drops to −90 (discounted by γ, blended by α). The penalty at w starts propagating backward into neighbour x — the core TD idea. Q-table: w = −100, x = (−90, 0), y=(0,0), z=0
"And so on…"
The deck signals this repeats across many steps/episodes: the −100 keeps propagating one state further back per episode, until every Q-value along w-x-y-z converges to an accurate estimate — with no environment model required.
🔎 Deep Dive — the classic "Cliff Walking" example (Sutton & Barto, Ex. 6.6)

A bigger, more dramatic version of the same grid: a 4×12 world where the agent starts bottom-left and must reach a goal bottom-right, with the entire bottom row between them being a cliff (huge penalty + reset).

SARSA learns a path that hugs the top of the grid, safely away from the cliff — because its update accounts for the actual next action it will take, including occasional random ε-greedy missteps. Q-learning (next section) learns the shortest path, walking right along the cliff's edge, assuming it will always act optimally going forward — faster/shorter, but riskier during training, since it occasionally still falls off due to its own exploration.

Real-world takeaway: for a physical robot where mistakes are costly (an arm near fragile equipment, a self-driving car in traffic), SARSA's cautious on-policy learning is usually preferred. In pure simulation, where "falling off a cliff" just means restarting an episode, Q-learning's more aggressive off-policy learning toward the true optimum is usually preferred.

04Off-Policy TD Control

Q-Learning — bootstrapping off the best possible next action

Q-learning looks almost identical to SARSA, with one crucial change: instead of using the Q-value of the actual next action chosen, it uses the max over all next actions — maxa Q(S′,a). It always assumes it will act greedily in the future, even if it's currently exploring ε-greedily. That's why it's off-policy: it learns the optimal policy's values while following a different, exploratory one.

Q-Learning update rule Q(S,A) ← Q(S,A) + α [ R + γ·maxa Q(S′,a) − Q(S,A) ]

Note the tuple only needs 4 items — (S, A, R, S′) — no next action required, since Q-learning uses "max" instead of "whatever we happened to pick".

Q-Learning (off-policy TD control) for estimating π ≈ π*
Initialize Q(s,a) arbitrarily, Q(terminal-state, ·) = 0
Repeat (for each episode):
    Initialize S
    Repeat (for each step of episode):
        Choose A from S using policy derived from Q (e.g., ε-greedy)
        Take action A, observe R, S'
        Q(S,A) ← Q(S,A) + α[R + γ·max_a Q(S',a) − Q(S,A)]
        S ← S'
    until S is terminal

Worked walkthrough — the treasure/pirate grid

New grid, same shape: w = treasure chest (+10), z = pirate (−100). Robot starts at x. Tracking tuple is now (S, A, R, S′) — no A′.

Step 1
Robot reaches z (the pirate), receiving −100. Q(z) updates to −100 — the bad terminal outcome in this grid. Q-table: w=0, x=(0,0), y=(0,0), z = −100
Step 2 (next episode)
Because taking a certain action from y eventually leads toward −100 at z, Q(y) for that action drops to −90 — the same discounted-bootstrapping propagation seen with SARSA, now for Q-learning. Q-table: w=0, x=(0,0), y = (0, −90), z = −100
"And so on…"
Over many episodes the −100 penalty keeps propagating backward through y, then x, then w — Q-learning building an accurate map of danger without ever needing a model of the environment.
🔎 Deep Dive — why Q-learning was such a big deal

Q-learning (Watkins, 1989) directly approximates the optimal action-value function q*, independent of the policy being followed. Decoupling "learning about the optimal policy" from "behaving exploratorily" dramatically simplified theoretical analysis and made early convergence proofs possible.

Modern relevance: Q-learning is the conceptual ancestor of Deep Q-Networks (DQN) — the algorithm DeepMind used in 2015 to reach human-level performance on dozens of Atari 2600 games directly from pixels. DQN is essentially tabular Q-learning's maxa Q(S′,a) update, with a neural network replacing the table so it generalises across huge numbers of possible screens.

05A Middle Ground

Expected SARSA

Instead of the value of "the action I actually took next" (SARSA) or "the single best next action" (Q-learning's max), Expected SARSA takes a probability-weighted average over all next actions — reducing the variance that comes from randomly sampling just one.

Expected SARSA update rule Q(St,At) ← Q(St,At) + α [ Rt+1 + γ 𝔼π[Q(St+1,At+1) | St+1] − Q(St,At) ]
  = Q(St,At) + α [ Rt+1 + γ Σa π(a|St+1) Q(St+1,a) − Q(St,At) ]
🔎 Deep Dive — when does Expected SARSA shine?

Averaging over all next actions eliminates the variance from randomly sampling one — in practice this often permits a larger step size α and smoother learning for the same experience, at the cost of looping over all actions per step instead of one lookup.

A neat theoretical fact: set the target policy inside the expectation to be greedy instead of ε-greedy, and Expected SARSA's formula becomes mathematically identical to Q-learning's update — Q-learning is really a special case of Expected SARSA, where the expectation collapses onto a single best action.

06A Subtle Failure Mode

Maximization Bias & Double Learning

Methods like Q-learning that use max over noisy estimates have a problem: taking the maximum of several noisy values tends to be systematically too optimistic — this is maximization bias. The fix is Double Learning: keep two Q-tables, use one to pick the best action and the other to evaluate how good it actually is. This cancels the bias, doubling memory but not per-step computation.

🔎 Deep Dive — the classic Sutton & Barto maximization-bias example

A tiny MDP: from state A, go Left or Right. Right ends the episode immediately with reward 0. Left goes to state B, from which many actions all end the episode with reward drawn from a distribution averaging −0.1. The true expected value of Left is −0.1 (worse than Right's guaranteed 0), so the optimal choice is always Right.

But early in learning, with only a few samples, some of B's random rewards happen to come out positive by chance. Plain Q-learning takes the max over B's noisy estimates — very likely one of the lucky positive-looking ones — so it wrongly thinks Left looks great, taking many extra episodes to unlearn the mistake.

Double Q-learning (Hasselt, 2010) fixes this with two independent tables Qa and Qb: one table selects the best action (argmax), the other evaluates it. Since the two tables have independent noise, a "lucky" overestimation in one is unlikely to also be selected by the other — the bias mostly cancels, and research shows Double Q-learning can even slightly underestimate, a far safer failure mode.

Modern relevance: this scaled up became Double DQN (van Hasselt, Guez & Silver, 2016), fixing a well-documented overestimation problem in DeepMind's original DQN on Atari, leading to more stable training and better final scores — same fix, with neural networks instead of tables.

07A Tunable Dial

n-step TD Prediction

TD(0) only looks one step ahead before bootstrapping; Monte Carlo waits for the entire episode. n-step TD generalises the pattern in between — look ahead 1, 2, 3, … or n steps of real reward before bootstrapping off your value estimate.

One and two-step returns Gt:t+1 = Rt+1 + γVt(St+1)   (this is TD(0))
Gt:t+2 = Rt+1 + γRt+2 + γ2Vt+1(St+2)
Spectrum of TD methods: 1-step bootstraps immediately; ∞-step / Monte Carlo uses the full episode return. (Ref: Sutton & Barto, §7.1)
General n-step return & update Gt:t+n = Rt+1 + γRt+2 + ⋯ + γn−1Rt+n + γnVt+n−1(St+n)
Vt+n(St) ← Vt+n−1(St) + α [ Gt:t+n − Vt+n−1(St) ]

Set n=1 and you get TD(0); let n→∞ and you get Monte Carlo.

n-step TD for estimating V ≈ vπ
Input: a policy π
Algorithm parameters: step size α ∈ (0,1], positive integer n
Initialize V(s) arbitrarily, for all s ∈ S
(store/access ops for S_t, R_t can take index mod n+1)

Loop for each episode:
    Initialize and store S_0 ≠ terminal
    T ← ∞
    Loop for t = 0, 1, 2, ...:
        If t < T:
            Take action per π(·|S_t); observe/store R_{t+1}, S_{t+1}
            If S_{t+1} is terminal, T ← t+1
        τ ← t − n + 1   (state whose estimate is being updated)
        If τ ≥ 0:
            G ← Σ_{i=τ+1}^{min(τ+n,T)} γ^{i-τ-1} R_i
            If τ+n < T: G ← G + γ^n V(S_{τ+n})
            V(S_τ) ← V(S_τ) + α[G − V(S_τ)]
    Until τ = T − 1

The tricky part: you always update a state from n steps in the past (τ = t − n + 1), because you need n future rewards before computing its n-step return. A rolling buffer of the last n+1 rewards/states makes this tractable.

🔎 Deep Dive — why TD(0) alone can be slow

Sutton & Barto call plain TD(0)'s limitation "the tyranny of the single timestep" — because it only bootstraps one step ahead, a reward far in the future has to propagate backward one state at a time, needing many passes before distant states "feel" its effect. The grid examples above show exactly this: the −100/+10 rewards only reached one neighbouring state per episode.

The classic illustration is the 19-state random walk: a chain of 19 states with reward only at the ends. Empirically, intermediate values of n usually perform best — not n=1 (too biased/slow to propagate distant rewards) and not large n approaching Monte Carlo (too high variance). This is a direct demonstration of the bias-variance tradeoff.

Practical takeaway: modern deep RL reuses this as "n-step returns" in Rainbow DQN and A3C/A2C, where a moderate n (commonly 3–5) instead of pure 1-step TD or full MC returns is a standard, well-tested speed-up.

Sources: medium.com/@physynapse — Tyranny of the Timestep · arxiv.org/pdf/2402.03903

RCRace Car — A Worked Incremental Example

One scenario, every method: MDP → DP → MC → TD → SARSA → Expected SARSA → Q-Learning → Double Q-Learning

A second deck (BITS Pilani WILP DRL team) walks a single running scenario through every method taught in the course, with real hand-computed numeric updates at each step. The story: a race car completing laps while managing engine temperature and speed decisions.

Problem definition — Race Car Laps

A race car trades lap time against overheating risk each segment. More laps completed is better; the car shuts down only if the engine overheats (an optional terminal state). The agent acts under uncertainty with only local percepts, and next engine temperature depends only on the current state — the Markov property holds in this framing.

MDP formalisation (simplified — used for DP)

ComponentDefinition
States S{Cool (C), Warm (W), Overheated (O)} — engine temperature, finite state MDP
Actions A{Go Slow (S), Go Fast (F)}
Rewards RR(s,a,s′) = +1 if reached by Slow; +2 if reached by Fast; −10 if s′ is terminal (Overheated)
Transitions PP(s′|s,a) = 0.5 if the action leads to a different non-terminal state; 1 otherwise

Example: Fast from Warm always (P=1.0) sends the car to Overheated with reward −10 — a guaranteed-crash scenario built in for teaching. Since P and R are fully known here, this version is the ideal setup for Dynamic Programming.

RCDynamic Programming

A known model — plan without ever driving

In this version, the agent is given a known map and risk factors in advance (a known model). With full knowledge, the optimal policy can be computed by planning across all states — never needing to actually drive.

Policy Iteration (PI)

  • Prefer for smaller state spaces
  • Intermediate termination gives an explicit, interpretable policy
  • Idea: one-step lookahead → Evaluate → Improve

Value Iteration (VI)

  • Prefer for larger state spaces
  • Fast convergence to the optimal policy at the end
  • Idea: one-step lookahead → Control (evaluation + improvement combined)

VI can be thought of as truncated PI — evaluation is done only partially (one sweep) before improving, rather than fully.

RCMonte Carlo

An unknown model — learn by driving full laps

Now the map may be known but the risk factors are not (an unknown model) — the impact of actions on engine state is hard to predict. With only partial knowledge, the agent computes a policy by running random experiments (sampling). Notably, this version explicitly relaxes the Markov assumption and adds a stopping condition (a lap limit), since MC needs episodes that terminate to compute a return.

Given: S, A, optionally R(s,a,s′)  ·  To find: policy π
Solution: sample full episodes; estimate values from discounted returns (simple / weighted / normalized weighted average)
On-policy: generate experience → evaluate using return → improve
Off-policy: use experience from a different behaviour policy → evaluate using return → improve

MC doesn't need the environment's rules — it runs full episodes and averages the actual rewards received to estimate how good each state or action was.

MDP formalisation (reduced), used from here on: states and actions stay the same, but rewards R and/or transition probabilities P may be non-stationary — no longer fixed or known in advance. A policy still needs V(S) and Q(S,A) estimated, but the process is not required to strictly follow the Markov property anymore. All that's ultimately needed is estimating value functions from experience.
RCTD(0) — Policy Evaluation, By Hand

TD(0) worked numerically on the race car

This version's model is unknown, like MC, but there's no lap limit — the race can continue indefinitely, and an immediate reward is available at every step, which is exactly what lets TD bootstrap step by step instead of waiting for termination.

Given: S, A, R(s,a,s′), optionally P(a|s)  ·  To find: policy π
Solution: sample episodes; compute value estimates using discounted next-state estimates (bootstrapping) — simple / weighted / normalized weighted average
On-policy: generate experience → evaluate using bootstrapping → improve  ·  Off-policy: use experience from another behaviour policy → evaluate using bootstrapping → improve

This slide is near-identical in structure to Monte Carlo's template two slides back — the one substitution is "Evaluate using Return" (MC) → "Evaluate using bootstrapping" (TD). That single swap is the entire conceptual difference between the two families.

Important note from the slide

Even though this deck shows the transition dynamics (e.g. a 0.5 chance of a different state), TD(0) itself never actually uses those probabilities — it only ever sees one sampled outcome per step, exactly like Monte Carlo. The dynamics are shown purely for the reader to follow along and check the numbers. In practice, TD works model-free, just like Monte Carlo.

Use case: a driving policy is already learnt and needs to be evaluated — for monitoring, or certification/approval by safety engineers.

Setup: V0 = [0, 0, 0] for [C, W, O]. Hyperparameters: ε = 0.1, γ = 0.9, α = 0.5.

Ep.1 · t0
Trajectory so far: C, Fast, +2, W. Target = R + γV(W) = 2 + 0.9×0 = 2. TD error = 2 − V(C) = 2 − 0 = 2. V(C) = 0 + 0.5(2) = 1  →  V₀ row: [1, 0, 0]
Ep.1 · t1
Continuing: …W, Slow, +1, C. V(W) = 0 + 0.5[1 + (0.9×1) − 0] = 0.95. Both the reward and Cool's freshly-updated value feed into Warm's update — bootstrapping in action. V₀ row: [1, 0.95, 0]
Ep.2
Trajectory: C,F,2,C,F,2,W,S,1,W.
V(C) = 1 + 0.5[2 + 0.9(1) − 1] = 1.95
V(C) = 1.95 + 0.5[2 + 0.9(0.95) − 1.95] ≈ 2.40
V(W) = 0.95 + 0.5[1 + 0.9(0.95) − 0.95] ≈ 1.40 V₁ row: [1.95, 2.40, 1.40]

Values climb episode over episode as the agent keeps experiencing the +2 reward for Fast-from-Cool — continuous small corrections, no waiting for a "final score". With more episodes these settle toward their true values under the policy being evaluated.

Practical note on ending episodes: since this version has no natural lap limit, an artificial cutoff (e.g. after 2 or 10 steps, or when Overheated occurs) is used so training can proceed in a structured way.
RCSARSA — On-Policy Control, By Hand

Watching variance build up, episode by episode

Q-table initialised to zero for all 6 (state, action) pairs. Hyperparameters: ε = 0.1, γ = 0.9, α = 0.8 (bigger than TD(0)'s 0.5).

Use case called out on the slide

A driving policy is already learnt, but conditions have drifted — the policy needs retraining to reflect the actual risk observed via exploration. SARSA (on-policy) suits this well, since it directly incorporates the risk of its own imperfect ε-greedy exploration.

Ep.1
(S,A,R,S′,A′) = (C, F, 2, W, S). Q(C,F) = 0 + 0.8[2 + 0.9×0 − 0] = 1.6. Next: Q(W,S) = 0 + 0.8[1 + 0.9×1.6 − 0] ≈ 1.95. Q row [C-F, C-S, W-F, W-S, O-F, O-S] = [1.6, 0, 0, 1.95, 0, 0]
Ep.2
Longer episode ending with Fast-from-Warm → Overheated (−10). t0: Q(C,F)=1.6+0.8[2+0.9(1)−1.6]≈3.07 · t1: Q(C,F)≈3.62 · t2: Q(W,S)=1.95+0.8[1+0.9(1.95)−1.95]≈2.59 · t3: Q(W,S) drops to ≈1.32 (stale Q(W,F)=0 mixed with a smaller reward) · t4: Q(W,F)=0+0.8[−10+0]=−8. Q row: [3.62, 0, −8, 1.32, 0, 0]
Ep.3–4 · Variance callout

RCExpected SARSA, By Hand

Same story, steadier numbers

Same starting Q-table and hyperparameters as SARSA (ε=0.1, γ=0.9, α=0.8), same trajectories — but every update averages over next actions instead of sampling one. For simplicity, all actions from Warm are assumed equiprobable in this episode.

Ep.1
Q(C,F) = 0+0.8[2+0.9(0.5×Q(W,S)+0.5×Q(W,F))−0] = 1.6 (identical to SARSA's first step, since both next-state values are still 0). Q(W,S) = 0+0.8[1+0.9(0.5×Q(C,S)+0.5×Q(C,F))−0] ≈ 1.37 — already more conservative than SARSA's 1.95. Q row: [1.6, 0, 0, 1.37, 0, 0]
Ep.2
Q(W,S) moves smoothly: 1.37 → 1.57 → 1.68. Even after Q(W,F) crashes to −8 (the Overheated outcome), Q(W,S) barely reacts — Expected SARSA averages Fast's badness in with a fixed weight rather than being fully at the mercy of whichever action got sampled. Q row: [2.59, 0, −8, 1.57, 0] Note on the slide: continuing for one more episode, likelihood is simplified as P(Slow|·)=0.05, P(Fast|·)=0.5 for the expectation calc (illustrative, not necessarily normalised).
Ep.3–4
Q(W,S): −1.14 → +2.46. Compare directly to SARSA's −4.69 → +4.47 over the same episodes — noticeably calmer.

RCQ-Learning, By Hand

Watching maximization bias appear in real numbers

Same starting Q-table and hyperparameters (ε=0.1, γ=0.9, α=0.8). Callout: Target policy = Greedy (MAX), used to evaluate/update Q-values; Behavior policy = ε-greedy, used to actually select actions.

Ep.1
Q(C,F) = 1.6 (identical to SARSA's first step — both next-state values still 0). Then, using an unusually large illustration reward of +20: Q(W,S) = 0+0.8[20+0.9×MAX{Q(C,S),Q(C,F)}−0] ≈ 17.15 — the max operator immediately reaping the benefit of the outsized reward. Q row: [1.6, 0, 0, 17.15, 0, 0]
Ep.2
Trajectory: C,F,2,C,F,4,W,S,−5,W,S,1,W,F,−10,O.
t0: Q(C,F) = 1.6+0.8(2+0.9×MAX{1.6,0}−1.6) ≈ 3.07
t1: Q(C,F) = 3.07+0.8(4+0.9×MAX{0,17.15}−3.07) ≈ 16.16 — watch this jump: because Q(W,S) was inflated to 17.15 last episode, and Q-learning always bootstraps off the max next value, that inflated number gets pulled forward into Q(C,F) too, even though the actual reward this step was a modest +4.
t2: Q(W,S) = 17.15+0.8(−5+0.9×MAX{0,17.15}−17.15) ≈ 11.78
t3: Q(W,S) = 11.78+0.8(1+0.9×MAX{0,11.78}−11.78) ≈ 11.64
t4: Q(W,F) = 0+0.8(−10+0.9×0−0) ≈ −8 Updated Q row: [16.16, 0, −8, 11.64, 0, 0]
Ep.3–6 · Maximization bias
Q(W,S) across these episodes: 14.71 → 22.50 → 16.70 → 19.37 — bouncing around, never quite settling.
Callout box (verbatim intent)

"Observe the difference in value updates w.r.t. Q(W,S) between successive time steps in episodes 3 to 6. The true value is approx. Q(W,S) = 15. Due to MAX bias the values are overestimated, and this is more pronounced in the presence of noise. Sometimes this diverges from the true value. Solution to tackle: Double Q-Learning. But in a near-deterministic, less noisy environment, Q-learning helps in faster convergence!"

This is a real, honest tradeoff — not a flaw to always avoid. In cleaner, near-deterministic environments, Q-learning's aggressive max-based updates actually converge faster; the bias problem specifically bites hardest when rewards or transitions carry meaningful noise.

RCDouble Q-Learning, By Hand

Two tables, cancelling each other's optimism

Two Q-tables side by side: Q2 ("Target"), initialised to all zeros, and Q1 ("Behavior"), pre-populated with [5, 1, 5, 15] — representing prior/logged experience. Hyperparameters: ε=0.1, γ=0.9, α=0.8.

Double Q-Learning
Initialize Q1(s,a) and Q2(s,a), such that Q(terminal,·) = 0
Loop for each episode:
    Initialize S
    Loop for each step:
        Choose A from S using policy ε-greedy in Q1 + Q2
        Take action A, observe R, S'
        With 0.5 probability:
            Q1(S,A) ← Q1(S,A) + α(R + γQ2(S', argmax_a Q1(S',a)) − Q1(S,A))
        else:
            Q2(S,A) ← Q2(S,A) + α(R + γQ1(S', argmax_a Q2(S',a)) − Q2(S,A))
        S ← S'
    until S is terminal
Ep.1 · Update 1
Q2(C,F) = 0.8[2 + 0.9×Q1(W, argmaxaQ2(W,a))] = 0.8[2 + 0.9×Q1(W,F)] (Q2's values at W are tied at 0, so F is picked arbitrarily) = 0.8[2 + 0.9×5] = 5.2. Note the "pick with one table, evaluate with the other" mechanic: Q2 picks the action (via its own argmax), but Q1's value is what's actually used to compute the target.
Ep.1 · Update 2
Q2(W,S) = 0.8[20 + 0.9×Q1(C, argmaxaQ2(C,a))] = 0.8[20 + 0.9×Q1(C,F)] = 0.8[20+0.9×5] ≈ 19.6. The episode terminates after 2 steps.
Ep.2 · Coin-flip alternates
t0: Q2(C,F) ≈ 6.24. t1: the coin-flip this time lands on Q1 instead — Q1(C,F) = 5+0.8(4+0.9×Q2(W, argmax{Q1(W,S),Q1(W,F)})−5) = 5+0.8(4+0.9×Q2(W,S)−5) = 5+0.8(4+0.9×19.6−5) ≈ 18.3. The two tables are already shaping each other's learning.
🔎 Deep Dive — the payoff, in one chart

Across episodes 3–6, plain Q-learning's Q(W,S) swings between ≈14.7 and ≈22.5 against a true value of ≈15. Double Q-learning's Q2 (target) table across the same episodes: 19.6 → 13.74 → 13.74 → 15.71 → 10.57 → 13.7 — hugging the true value far more closely, with none of the wild spikes.

Maximization bias comparison — Q(Warm,Slow) vs. the ≈15 true baseline. Q-learning overshoots and swings; Double Q-learning tracks the baseline much more tightly.

×Cross-Cutting

Conceptual Deep Dives

A · On-policy vs. off-policy, made concrete

SARSA and Expected SARSA use the same policy for behaviour and evaluation. Q-learning and Double Q-learning explicitly separate a behaviour policy (ε-greedy, for acting) from a target policy (greedy/max, or split across two tables, for updating). The race car's SARSA-vs-Q-learning worked examples (both starting identically at Q=1.6 for the first update) only diverge once next-state Q-values become unequal — that's the moment on-policy and off-policy start telling different stories.

B · Variance (SARSA) vs. Maximization Bias (Q-learning) — the deck's two signature demonstrations

SARSA's Q-values swing wildly because they depend on whichever next action was randomly sampled. Q-learning's Q-values systematically overshoot because taking a max over noisy estimates is optimistically biased. Two different failure modes, two different fixes: Expected SARSA (averages away variance) and Double Learning (separates selection from evaluation to cancel bias).

C · Bootstrapping, everywhere

Every update in this session — TD(0), SARSA, Expected SARSA, Q-learning, Double Q-learning, n-step TD — shares one structure: estimate ← estimate + α[target − estimate], where the target itself leans on another (possibly still-imperfect) estimate. This is bootstrapping, and it's the single idea that makes step-by-step, model-free, online learning possible.

D · From TD(0) to Monte Carlo — a spectrum, not a binary choice

n-step TD reframes "TD vs. MC" as two ends of a dial rather than a hard choice: n=1 gives TD(0) (biased but low-variance), n→∞ gives Monte Carlo (unbiased but high-variance), and intermediate n — as the 19-state random walk demonstrates — is usually the sweet spot. This is the same lever Rainbow DQN and A3C/A2C reach for today.
