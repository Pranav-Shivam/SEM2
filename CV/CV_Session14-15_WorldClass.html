01

Motion Detection vs Object Tracking

CV15 Pages 1-2

Intuition first — following an ant, not just spotting one

Object tracking is the process of locating moving objects in a video over time — the follow-up problem to detection. Detection finds "what and where" in a single frame; tracking keeps that same object's identity continuous as it moves across many frames, like keeping your finger on a specific ant as it walks across a table rather than just spotting "there is an ant" in one photo. Applications: autonomous vehicles (predicting where a pedestrian will be next), surveillance (following a person across camera frames), and augmented reality (keeping a virtual object glued to a real surface as the camera moves).

Key difference — sensor trigger vs algorithmic identity

Motion DetectionObject Tracking
MechanismSensors (infrared, microwave) or pixel differencingAlgorithms over a video sequence
Answers"Something moved" (a trigger)"This object is here, moving this way"
IdentityNone — no notion of which objectMaintained across frames
Example useAuto-doors, security lightsTraffic camera reporting "car #42, 65 km/h northbound"

Two classic camera-based motion cues

  1. Frame differencing

    Subtract the current frame from the previous one, pixel by pixel — large difference = motion. Cheap, but very sensitive to noise and lighting flicker; fails on slow or uniformly-colored objects.

  2. Background subtraction (GMM)

    Model the static background per-pixel as a mixture of Gaussians (allowing for swaying leaves, lighting flicker), then flag any pixel that doesn't fit the model as foreground. More robust than frame differencing.

Exam Radar

Motion detection — challenges & gotchas

  • Both methods share the same weaknesses: noise/lighting changes create false "motion," and a moving camera makes the entire frame appear to change — they assume a mostly-static background.
  • Common mistake: treating "detection" and "tracking" as interchangeable — detection alone re-identifies objects independently every frame with zero identity continuity.
  • "Background subtraction" is a family of techniques (running average, GMM/MoG, adaptive GMM, deep FgSegNet), not one fixed algorithm.
Research Enhanced

GMM background subtraction traces to Stauffer & Grimson (MIT, 1999), "Adaptive background mixture models for real-time tracking" — models each pixel's intensity over time as a mixture of Gaussians. It's a direct ancestor of OpenCV's BackgroundSubtractorMOG2, still used today in traffic-flow monitoring and video surveillance. PTZ/drone cameras can't use naive background subtraction since the background itself shifts every frame — production systems compensate with camera-motion (ego-motion) registration first. Kwak, Lim, Nam, Han & Han (ICCV 2011) generalize the basic GMM model further with belief propagation and Bayesian filtering, handling the harder case where the camera itself moves slightly or the background's appearance drifts over time.

Stauffer, Grimson, "Adaptive background mixture models for real-time tracking," CVPR 1999 · Kwak, Lim, Nam, Han, Han, "Generalized background subtraction based on hybrid inference by belief propagation and Bayesian filtering," ICCV 2011

02

Tracking-by-Detection & Its Challenges

CV15 Page 3

Intuition first — detect every frame, then link the dots

The dominant, most intuitive tracking strategy: run an object detector (YOLO, SSD) independently on every single frame, then solve a matching problem — "which detection in frame 2 corresponds to which detection in frame 1?" — using a similarity metric (position/size closeness, visual similarity, or both). Like a security guard glancing at 30 photographs taken a second apart, circling every person in each, then figuring out which circle in photo 5 is the same person circled in photo 4.

Pipeline

  1. Detect

    Run a detector (YOLO / SSD) on every frame independently — no memory of previous frames yet.

  2. Predict

    Estimate where each existing track should be this frame (constant-velocity motion model, or a Kalman filter — Section 06).

  3. Match

    Solve the association problem between predicted track positions and new detections, typically via a similarity metric and bipartite matching (the Hungarian algorithm).

  4. Update / spawn / drop

    Matched tracks get their state updated; unmatched detections spawn new tracks; unmatched tracks age out (or wait, to survive brief occlusion).

Exam Radar

The three classic failure modes

  • Occlusion — an object briefly hidden behind a pole isn't detected for a few frames, breaking the chain; the tracker must "remember" and re-associate on reappearance.
  • Fast-moving objects — large frame-to-frame displacement defeats a naive "nearest position" similarity metric.
  • Distractors — rain, snow, camera shake, illumination changes create spurious detections/movements that confuse both detection and linking.
Research Enhanced

SORT (Simple Online and Realtime Tracking, 2016) links detections purely by motion — a Kalman filter predicts the next position, boxes matched by IoU — fast, but struggles badly with occlusion since a motion-only metric has nothing to match against once a detection vanishes. DeepSORT (2017) adds a learned appearance embedding (CNN re-identification features) as a second similarity cue, substantially cutting identity switches during brief occlusions at some cost to speed. Both remain the reference pipeline for modern multi-object tracking (MOT): detect → predict → match (Hungarian algorithm) → update.

Bewley et al., "Simple Online and Realtime Tracking," ICIP 2016 · Wojke, Bewley, Paulus, "Simple Online and Realtime Tracking with a Deep Association Metric," ICIP 2017

Why This Matters — a different, complementary idea is coming

Tracking-by-detection works at the level of whole boxes. Optical flow, next, is a different idea entirely: instead of detecting a whole object and matching boxes, it looks at raw pixel-level motion between two consecutive frames — "how did each little patch of the image move?" That raw motion feeds motion detection, tracking, and scene understanding alike.

03

Optical Flow & the Aperture Problem

CV15 Pages 9-13

Intuition first — a vector field of "where did this brightness go?"

Optical flow is the apparent motion of brightness patterns in the image — a vector field: at every pixel, an arrow showing how far and in what direction that patch's brightness appears to have moved. The word "apparent" matters: apparent motion can be caused by lighting changes without any actual motion — a rotating barber pole shows only vertical apparent motion despite rotating around its own axis, and a stationary shiny sphere under a moving light source shows apparent flow ("moving highlights") with zero actual physical motion. Optical flow measures brightness motion, not guaranteed physical motion.

Two broad ways to recover motion

Optical flow is dense — estimate a motion vector at every single pixel. Feature-tracking is sparse — find a handful of distinctive points (Shi-Tomasi corners, Section 03's eigenvalue test) and follow only those across frames (Lucas-Kanade, Section 04). Dense is complete but expensive; sparse is fast but only as good as its chosen points.

Three named methods — global, local, dense

The deck names three classical optical-flow algorithms and explicitly frames them by scope, a frequently-tested comparison:

MethodScopeIdea
Horn-SchunckGlobalAssumes motion changes smoothly across the whole image — solves for flow everywhere via a global smoothness constraint. Good at complete, smooth fields; can blur across true motion boundaries.
Lucas-KanadeLocalAssumes motion is constant within a small window (Section 04) — fast, great for tracking distinctive corner points; doesn't naturally give flow everywhere.
FarnebackDenseEstimates a flow vector for every pixel using local polynomial approximations — OpenCV's cv2.calcOpticalFlowFarneback.

These aren't really competitors — Bruhn, Weickert & Schnörr's "Lucas/Kanade Meets Horn/Schunck" (2005) shows combining the local method's robustness with the global method's density can outperform either alone. They're different points on the same local-vs-global, sparse-vs-dense spectrum.

Brightness constancy → the optical flow constraint equation
Brightness of a tracked point stays constant as it moves by (u,v) over δt=1: I(x, y, t−1) = I(x+u, y+v, t) Taylor-linearize the right side, cancel I(x,y,t−1) from both sides: Ixu + Iyv + It ≈ 0 ≡ ∇I·[u v]T + It = 0 Assumptions baked in: brightness constancy, small motion (justifies Taylor expansion), spatial coherence.

The aperture problem — one equation, two unknowns

The constraint equation is a single scalar equation in two unknowns (u, v) — underdetermined at one pixel, like solving "3 = a + b" for both a and b: infinitely many solutions lie along a line in (u,v) space. Viewing a moving edge through a tiny peephole, you can tell how fast it slides perpendicular to itself, but not whether it's also sliding along its own length — that ambiguity is the aperture problem, classically demonstrated by the barber-pole illusion.

Escaping the aperture problem — classify the neighborhood first

Whether a point is even trackable depends on its local structure. Build the 2nd-moment matrix M = Σ[[Ix², IxIy],[IxIy, Iy²]] over a small window (same matrix used by the Harris corner detector) and look at its eigenvalues λ₁, λ₂:

EigenvaluesLocal structureTrackable?
Both large, comparableCornerYes — motion fully determined in both directions
One large, one smallEdgeOnly perpendicular to the edge (aperture problem)
Both smallFlat regionNo — no gradient information at all

Shi-Tomasi (1994) criterion for a "good feature to track": min(λ₁, λ₂) is large — exactly what OpenCV's cv2.goodFeaturesToTrack computes.

04

Lucas-Kanade — Windowed Least Squares & Pyramids

CV15 Pages 4, 11-16

Intuition first — borrow equations from your neighbors

Lucas & Kanade's fix for the aperture problem: assume a small window of neighboring pixels shares the same (u,v) — the spatial coherence assumption. A 5×5 window gives 25 stacked copies of the brightness-constancy equation, all sharing two unknowns: an overconstrained system solvable by least squares.

Overconstrained system → least-squares solution
Stack the constraint for every pixel pi in a K×K window: A d = b A = [Ix(pi), Iy(pi)] (K²×2) d = [u, v]T b = −It(pi) (K²×1) Least-squares solution (this IS the 2nd-moment matrix M from Section 03): d = (ATA)−1 ATb — solvable exactly when ATA is well-conditioned, i.e. at a corner.

Pyramidal (coarse-to-fine) Lucas-Kanade

LK's "small motion" assumption usually breaks — real motion is often several pixels, not a fraction of one. Fix: build a Gaussian pyramid of both frames, run LK at the coarsest level first (where the same physical motion looks smaller), scale the estimate ×2 as a warm-start for the next finer level, and repeat down to full resolution.

Exam Radar

Lucas-Kanade — exam radar

  • Three assumptions to name on demand: brightness constancy, small motion, spatial coherence.
  • Why LK needs a window at all: 1 equation / 2 unknowns per pixel (aperture problem) → K² equations / 2 unknowns per window (solvable).
  • Pyramidal LK doesn't change the per-level math — it only changes what counts as "small motion" by working at a downsampled scale first.
Research Enhanced

Lucas & Kanade, IJCAI 1981, remains the basis of OpenCV's cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK (pyramidal formulation from Bouguet's tech report) — still the standard sparse, feature-point tracker used in visual SLAM and AR today. FlowNet (2015) was the first deep-learned dense optical flow network, trained on the synthetic "Flying Chairs" dataset since real dense-flow ground truth is nearly impossible to collect; successors PWC-Net and RAFT now dominate flow benchmarks and feed applications like autonomous driving (motion cues for obstacle prediction) and medical imaging (tissue motion analysis), but the classical LK pyramid is still preferred when speed and interpretability matter more than raw accuracy.

Lucas, Kanade, "An iterative image registration technique...," IJCAI 1981 · Bouguet, "Pyramidal Implementation of the Lucas Kanade Feature Tracker," Intel Tech Report

05

Mean-Shift & Camshift Tracking

CV15 Pages 7, 17-22

Intuition first — feel around for the warmest spot

Mean-shift gives up on a rigid motion model and tracks appearance instead — typically a color histogram. It's a mode-seeking algorithm: like feeling around in the dark for the warmest spot on a table and always taking a small step in the warmer direction until you settle on the hottest point. Non-parametric — no assumed distribution shape — and works in any feature space (color, texture, scale), which is what makes it reusable well beyond tracking. (Framework per R. Collins, CSE598G, Penn State, building on Comaniciu, Ramesh & Meer.)

The general appearance-tracking framework — two pipelines

The deck splits appearance-based tracking into two separate pipelines, then plugs mean-shift into the second one as just one of three interchangeable mode-seeking choices:

  1. Target Representation (build the model, once)

    Choose a reference target → choose a feature space (e.g. quantized color) → represent that target as a probability distribution (PDF) in that feature space.

  2. Target Localization (repeat every frame)

    Start at the last known position → search the neighborhood → find the candidate that maximizes similarity to the reference PDF → repeat next frame.

The "mode-seeking" step inside Target Localization can be any of three algorithms — mean-shift, Lucas-Kanade, or a particle filter — all solving "find the best-matching neighborhood location," just with different search strategies. This guide covers mean-shift here and Lucas-Kanade in Section 04.

The mean-shift iteration, step by step

  1. Place a window

    Start the search window at the object's last known location.

  2. Compute the center of mass

    Weight every pixel inside the window by how well it matches the target's color histogram (via back-projection); compute the weighted center of mass.

  3. Form the mean-shift vector

    vector = center-of-mass − window-center. This always points toward denser data — an estimate of the local density gradient (Fukunaga-Hostetler, 1975).

  4. Shift the window

    Move the window to the center of mass.

  5. Repeat until convergence

    Iterate 2-4 until the vector shrinks to ~zero — that's the mode (the best match for this frame).

Color histogram quantization & illumination invariance
Joint histogram (correlated channels), n-bit quantization per channel: (28−n)3 bins (4-bit → 4096 bins) Marginal histogram (3 separate 1-D): 3 × 28−n bins (4-bit → 48 bins — compact, less discriminative) Normalized (chromaticity) color — illumination-invariant: (r′,g′,b′) = (r,g,b) / (r+g+b) Localization similarity — Bhattacharyya coefficient between target (q̂) & candidate (p̂) histograms: ρ̂(y) = Σu √(p̂u(y) · q̂u) — higher ρ̂ = better color match; maximized over y to relocalize each frame.

Comaniciu, Ramesh & Meer weight pixels with an Epanechnikov kernel (a parabolic profile) rather than counting them equally — pixels near the window's edge are down-weighted, since they're more likely to belong to background or an occluder than pixels near the center.

Adaptive scale & Camshift

Plain mean-shift uses a fixed window size; to handle an object growing/shrinking, run localization at ~3 kernel sizes (h, smaller, larger) and keep whichever gives the best similarity score. Camshift ("Continuously Adaptive Mean-Shift") bakes this in — it lets the window resize and rotate as the tracked face gets closer/farther or tilts, rather than staying a fixed box. It's the algorithm behind OpenCV's classic face-tracking demo.

Exam Radar

Mean-shift — exam radar

  • Robust to partial occlusion, motion blur, and mild distraction (a similarly-colored nearby object) — fails on drastic appearance change (the model never updates what "the object" looks like).
  • Reported results (Comaniciu, Ramesh, Meer 2003): 16×16×16 quantized RGB, ~4 mean-shift iterations per frame to converge.
  • Common mistake: confusing mean-shift (appearance/histogram mode-seeking) with Lucas-Kanade (pixel-gradient motion) — they solve different problems and can be combined.
Research Enhanced

Camshift was introduced by Gary Bradski at Intel, 1998 ("Computer Vision Face Tracking for Use in a Perceptual User Interface") — one of OpenCV's earliest, most iconic demos precisely because it's fast, needs no training, and ran in real time on modest late-90s hardware. It's since been superseded in production by deep-learning re-identification trackers far more robust to occlusion and clutter, but remains a staple teaching example for exactly that reason.

Bradski, "Computer Vision Face Tracking for Use in a Perceptual User Interface," Intel Technology Journal, 1998 · Comaniciu, Ramesh, Meer, "Kernel-Based Object Tracking," IEEE TPAMI 2003

06

Kalman Filters — Predict / Correct

CV15 Pages 23-27

Intuition first — trust, but verify, every single frame

A Kalman filter is a recursive estimator: it predicts where an object should be next using a motion model, then corrects that prediction using a noisy measurement — weighting each source by how much it trusts it. Classic scenarios: a bird flitting through a forest, a radar tracking missile blips, or predicting a planet's next position. 1D constant-velocity model: xk = xk−1 + ẋk−1·Δt + noise.

The 5-step predict/correct cycle — memorize this order
1. Predict state: xp = F·xk−1 (+ B·u, control term, often dropped) 2. Predict covariance: Pp = F·Pk−1·FT + Q 3. Innovation: y = zk − H·xp (measurement minus prediction, in measurement space) 4. Kalman gain: K = Pp·HT·(H·Pp·HT + R)−1 5. Update: xk = xp + K·(zk − H·xp) ; Pk = Pp − K·H·Pp

Symbol table

SymbolMeaning
x, PState estimate, error covariance
FState transition / motion model
HMeasurement matrix (state → measurement space)
Q, RProcess noise, measurement noise (covariances)
KKalman gain — how much to trust the new measurement
zMeasurement; subscript p = predicted, k = corrected

Exam Radar

Kalman gain — exam radar

  • R large (noisy sensor) → K shrinks → filter trusts the prediction more. Q large (unpredictable motion) → K grows → filter trusts the new measurement more.
  • Order matters: predict state → predict covariance → innovation → gain → update. A common exam trap swaps steps 1 and 2.
  • Plain Kalman filters assume linear motion + Gaussian noise — nonlinear cases need the Extended or Unscented Kalman Filter.
Research Enhanced

Rudolf Kálmán published the filter in 1960; it flew on the Apollo Guidance Computer (championed by Stanley Schmidt at NASA Ames) to fuse noisy sensor readings into a real-time spacecraft trajectory estimate — one of the filter's first and most famous production deployments. Today it's the default state estimator wherever you have a predictable motion model and noisy sensors: GPS navigation, radar tracking, and — directly relevant here — as the motion-prediction core inside SORT and DeepSORT (Section 07).

Kálmán, "A New Approach to Linear Filtering and Prediction Problems," Journal of Basic Engineering, 1960

07

DNN-Based Trackers

CV15 Pages 27-28

Three modern approaches — and why they're hybrids, not replacements

DeepSORT extends SORT with a learned appearance re-identification embedding, giving it a second similarity cue beyond IoU/motion — but it still runs a Kalman filter internally for motion prediction, so it's a hybrid, not a wholesale replacement of the classical pipeline. FairMOT goes further, using a single network (CenterNet-based) to jointly produce detections and re-ID embeddings in one forward pass, rather than running a separate detector and a separate re-ID network. Siamese trackers (SiamFC, SiamRPN) learn a general patch-similarity function offline, then track online purely by searching each new frame for the best-matching patch to the target's first appearance.

Traditional vs DNN-based trackers

Traditional (Mean-Shift, KLT)DNN-based (DeepSORT, Siamese)
FeaturesHandcrafted (color histogram, gradients)Learned (CNN embeddings)
Occlusion handlingPoor — no memory of appearance changeGood — re-ID embedding survives brief occlusion
Speed / hardwareFast, CPU-friendlySlower, typically needs a GPU
TrainingNone requiredRequires labeled tracking/re-ID data
Research Enhanced

DeepSORT (Wojke, Bewley, Paulus, ICIP 2017) is the workhorse baseline in most modern multi-object-tracking pipelines specifically because it's a minimal, well-understood addition to SORT rather than a ground-up redesign — a good illustration of how production CV systems tend to evolve incrementally, layering a learned component onto a proven classical core (a Kalman filter) rather than discarding it.

Wojke, Bewley, Paulus, "Simple Online and Realtime Tracking with a Deep Association Metric," ICIP 2017

08

Applications Beyond Tracking

CV15 Pages 4-8

Motion feeds more than tracking

Once you can estimate motion, several other applications fall out for free: video quality (motion stabilization, multi-frame super-resolution), motion-based segmentation (group pixels that move together), and event/activity recognition (what's happening, not just what's there). A video is really a function of space and time, (x, y, t) — every technique on this page is a different way of analyzing how that function changes along the time axis.

Visual SLAM & multi-frame super-resolution

Visual SLAM (Simultaneous Localization And Mapping) solves a chicken-and-egg problem with only a camera: track visual features frame-to-frame to estimate how the camera moved, while simultaneously using those same points to build a 3D map — better mapping helps localization and vice versa. The deck's demo is credited to Jean-Yves Bouguet (Caltech Vision Lab). Multi-frame super-resolution combines several slightly-shifted, low-resolution frames of the same scene into one sharper image via a 4-step pipeline: register (find pixel correspondences) → warp (align to one reference frame) → fuse (combine) → deblur (optional sharpen). Each low-res frame contributes a slightly different sub-pixel sample of the true scene.

Motion segmentation, 3D localization, body parts & activity recognition

Motion segmentation splits a video into groups of pixels that move coherently — the car's pixels form one group, a pedestrian's another; Pundlik & Birchfield's "Motion Segmentation at Any Speed" (BMVC 2006) specifically handles objects moving at very different speeds in the same scene. Joint tracking + 3D localization (Choi & Savarese) upgrades a 2D box on screen to an estimated real-world position from a single, minimally-calibrated camera, solving tracking and 3D placement together since each helps disambiguate the other. Body-part tracking follows the articulated skeleton (head, torso, limbs) using a coarse-to-fine cascade — cheap detectors first narrow the search, expensive ones refine it, the same philosophy behind the Viola-Jones face detector and modern pose networks. Activity recognition represents a video clip as a "bag" of recurring motion-and-appearance patterns and can discover action categories (walking, running) without hand-labeled examples; group activity recognition (Choi & Savarese) goes further, labeling what a whole cluster of people is doing together using a short activity-label vocabulary — X=Crossing, S=Waiting, Q=Queuing, W=Walking, T=Talking, D=Dancing — e.g. a group's label sequence might read "Crossing – Talking – Queuing – Dancing – jogging" across a clip.

SIFT-based object tracking

For tracking a flat/planar object (a book cover, a poster) as it moves, rotates, or is seen from a new angle, SIFT (Scale Invariant Feature Transform) keypoints — distinctive points that survive scale and rotation change — get re-detected and matched frame to frame. Del Bimbo & Pernici's "Object Tracking by Oversampling Local Features" (TPAMI 2014, the "ALIEN" project) collects many overlapping keypoint descriptors over time rather than relying on one snapshot, then combines matches via a RANSAC voting scheme for robustness — like recognizing a landmark from many previously-seen angles rather than just one reference photo.

Research Enhanced

Multi-frame super-resolution traces to Irani & Peleg (1990) and was made practical for real video by Farsiu, Robinson, Elad & Milanfar (2004) — the same underlying idea behind Google's "Super Res Zoom" on Pixel phones, which fuses natural hand-tremor shifts between burst shots. It cannot invent detail that was never captured across any frame, despite the "enhance and zoom into the reflection" trope. Cascaded pose estimation (Sapp, Toshev, Taskar, ECCV 2010) prefigures the coarse-to-fine strategies in OpenPose and stacked-hourglass networks; unsupervised activity recognition (Niebles, Wang, Fei-Fei, BMVC 2006) borrowed topic-modeling techniques (pLSA/LDA) straight from NLP, treating motion patterns as "visual words." Bouguet later led computer vision at Magic Leap, working on visual-inertial odometry — a direct commercial descendant of visual SLAM now powering AR headsets.

Irani, Peleg, "Super Resolution From Image Sequences," ICPR 1990 · Sapp, Toshev, Taskar, "Cascaded Models for Articulated Pose Estimation," ECCV 2010 · Niebles, Wang, Fei-Fei, "Unsupervised Learning of Human Action Categories," BMVC 2006 · Pundlik, Birchfield, "Motion Segmentation at Any Speed," BMVC 2006 · Choi, Savarese, "A Unified Framework for Multi-Target Tracking and Collective Activity Recognition," ECCV 2012 · Del Bimbo, Pernici, "Object Tracking by Oversampling Local Features," IEEE TPAMI 2014

Confusion Matrix & mAP — Worked Setup

CV16 Pages 29-34

The recap — confusion matrix, precision, recall, IoU

Every detection scoring pipeline starts from the confusion matrix: TP, FP, FN, TN. Precision = TP/(TP+FP) — "of what I retrieved, how much was relevant." Recall = TP/(TP+FN) — "of what was relevant, how much did I retrieve." A box counts as a match only if IoU ≥ 0.5 (the PASCAL VOC convention) — COCO instead averages over IoU thresholds 0.5 to 0.95.

The deck's mAP setup

The deck's worked example uses 24 ROI proposals against 15 ground-truth objects. Its per-row TP/FP table was rendered as an image in the source slide, not machine-readable text — so rather than invent specific numbers, here is the exact methodology it applies (matching Section 08 of the previous session's detection guide):

  1. Sort all detections by confidence, descending.

  2. Walk down the list; for each detection, greedily match it to the highest-IoU unclaimed ground-truth box if IoU ≥ 0.5.

  3. A detection matching an already-claimed ground-truth box (a duplicate) is scored FP, even if its own IoU clears the threshold.

  4. At each rank, compute cumulative precision = TP/(TP+FP) and recall = TP/(total GT).

  5. Interpolate the precision-recall curve and average it into one Average Precision number — 11-point interpolation (VOC2007) or all-point interpolation (VOC2010+, COCO).

⊞ Interactive · Toggle TP/FP, Watch AP Recompute

A smaller illustrative example (8 ranked detections, 5 ground-truth objects) applying the exact methodology above. Click any row to flip TP⇄FP — cumulative precision/recall and interpolated AP update live.
Exam Radar

mAP — exam radar

  • Remember the duplicate-detection rule — only the first (highest-confidence) match to a GT box counts as TP; later matches to the same box are FP.
  • COCO's lower-looking AP numbers vs VOC's mAP reflect a stricter IoU-averaging convention, not a worse model.
  • mAP = mean of per-class AP — "m" is doing real work; a single-class AP alone isn't mAP.
Research Enhanced

The 11-point vs all-point interpolation distinction matters historically: PASCAL VOC used 11-point interpolation through 2007, then switched to all-point (every unique recall value) from VOC2010 onward for a more exact area-under-curve estimate — COCO's evaluation protocol inherits the all-point convention, plus its signature IoU-threshold averaging.

Everingham et al., "The PASCAL Visual Object Classes Challenge: A Retrospective," IJCV 2015

ROC Analysis & AUC

CV16 Pages 34-37

Intuition first — a curve, not a single number

A ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curve plots True Positive Rate against False Positive Rate as the decision threshold sweeps from strict to lenient — showing the full trade-off a single accuracy number hides. The diagonal (TPR = FPR at every threshold) is the random-guess baseline: a coin flip.

TPR / FPR and the landmark points
TPR (y-axis) = TP / (TP+FN) = TP / P — fraction of actual positives caught FPR (x-axis) = FP / (FP+TN) = FP / N — fraction of actual negatives wrongly flagged (0,0) = never predicts positive  ·  (1,1) = always predicts positive  ·  (0,1) = perfect classifier

Reading the curve — the northwest rule

Point A is better than point B if A sits up-and-to-the-left of B (higher TPR and/or lower FPR) — the "northwest rule." Classifiers operating near the x-axis are conservative (need strong evidence, few false positives, but also catch fewer true positives); near the top-right they're liberal (weak evidence is enough, catch most true positives, but at the cost of more false positives). A discrete classifier (a decision tree) yields a single ROC point; a scoring classifier (Naive Bayes, a neural net) sweeps a threshold to trace out a full curve.

⊞ Interactive · Build the ROC Curve, One Instance at a Time

12 instances, pre-sorted by score descending. Step through them one at a time — positives push the curve up, negatives push it right. Watch the running AUC estimate converge.

The linear-scan algorithm behind the curve

Sort all instances by score, descending. Walk down the list once: on a true positive, step up (increment TP); on a true negative, step right (increment FP); plot (FP/N, TP/P) after each step. This exploits the fact that the curve is monotonic in threshold, giving an O(n log n) algorithm (dominated by the sort) instead of re-evaluating the whole dataset at every possible threshold.

Why This Matters — AUC as a single number

AUC (Area Under the Curve) equals the probability that the classifier ranks a randomly chosen positive instance above a randomly chosen negative one — the Mann-Whitney U statistic in disguise (Hanley & McNeil, 1982). Bigger AUC = better ranking ability, computable from the same linear scan via trapezoid-area accumulation, no separate pass required.

Research Enhanced

Nearly all of this ROC material follows Tom Fawcett's widely-cited tutorial, "An Introduction to ROC Analysis" (2006) — still the standard reference for practitioners. ROC analysis originated in WWII signal-detection theory (distinguishing radar echoes from noise) and was adopted by medicine (diagnostic test evaluation) well before machine learning; the AUC-as-ranking-probability interpretation is what makes it the metric of choice whenever class imbalance makes raw accuracy misleading — unlike accuracy, ROC/AUC don't change if the ratio of positives to negatives shifts.

Exam Quiz — 10 Questions

Q1EasyMotion vs tracking
A PIR sensor flips on a porch light when someone walks by. Is this object tracking?
  • AYes — it detected motion, which is the first step of tracking
  • BNo — it has no notion of object identity across time, just a motion trigger
  • CYes — infrared sensing is equivalent to a Kalman filter's measurement step
  • DNo — tracking requires a camera, and PIR sensors don't use one
Motion detection is a trigger with no identity continuity; tracking explicitly maintains "this is the same object" across frames. A conflates the two. C misapplies Kalman terminology. D is close but wrong for the reason — some tracking is camera-based, but the defining difference is identity, not sensor type.Common mistakeTreating "detected something moved" as equivalent to "tracking an object."
Q2EasyAperture problem
At a single pixel, the optical flow constraint equation Ixu + Iyv + It = 0 has how many equations and unknowns?
  • A1 equation, 2 unknowns
  • B2 equations, 2 unknowns
  • C1 equation, 1 unknown
  • D2 equations, 1 unknown
One scalar equation, two unknowns (u, v) — underdetermined, exactly the aperture problem. B and D wrongly assume two constraint equations exist per pixel. C forgets flow has both a u and a v component.Common mistakeForgetting that "solvable" requires a second constraint (spatial coherence, Section 04) — a single pixel alone never determines (u,v).
Q3EasyShi-Tomasi corners
Per the Shi-Tomasi criterion, which eigenvalue condition of the 2nd-moment matrix M marks a good feature to track?
  • Amax(λ₁, λ₂) is large
  • Bλ₁ and λ₂ have opposite signs
  • Cmin(λ₁, λ₂) is large
  • DBoth eigenvalues equal zero
min(λ₁,λ₂) large means BOTH directions have strong gradient information — a corner, fully trackable. A would also flag edges (one large eigenvalue is enough), which are only partially trackable. D describes a flat, untrackable region.Common mistakeUsing max() instead of min() — an edge has one large eigenvalue too, but is still aperture-problem-limited.
Q4MediumPyramidal LK
Why does pyramidal Lucas-Kanade process the coarsest pyramid level first, rather than starting at full resolution?
  • ACoarse levels have more corners to track
  • BThe same physical motion looks smaller (in pixels) at a downsampled scale, satisfying LK's small-motion assumption
  • CIt reduces the number of unknowns from 2 to 1
  • DCoarse levels have higher signal-to-noise ratio
A 10px full-res motion looks like ~1.25px at the coarsest level — small enough for LK's linearization to hold; the estimate is then upscaled ×2 as a warm-start into each finer level. C is false — still (u,v), two unknowns, at every level.Common mistakeThinking the pyramid changes the underlying math — it only changes what motion magnitude "counts as small" at each scale.
Q5MediumMean-shift vector
In mean-shift tracking, what does the mean-shift vector (window center → center of mass) approximate?
  • AThe local density gradient — it points toward denser data
  • BThe optical flow field at the window center
  • CThe Kalman gain for this frame
  • DThe camera's ego-motion between frames
The mean-shift vector estimates the density gradient direction (Fukunaga-Hostetler, 1975) — repeatedly stepping along it converges to a local mode (density peak). B confuses two unrelated tracking paradigms. C, D are unrelated concepts from other sections.Common mistakeAssuming mean-shift uses pixel-gradient motion like Lucas-Kanade — it uses appearance/histogram density, not brightness gradients.
Q6MediumKalman order
In the Kalman filter's 5-step cycle, what must be computed immediately before the Kalman gain K?
  • AThe corrected state xk
  • BThe corrected covariance Pk
  • CThe predicted state xp only
  • DThe predicted covariance Pp and the innovation y = zk − H·xp
Order: predict state → predict covariance → innovation → gain (needs Pp) → update. K = Pp·HT·(H·Pp·HT+R)−1 directly requires Pp. A, B are computed AFTER K, in the update step. C is necessary but not sufficient — Pp is also required.Common mistakeTrying to compute K before predicting covariance — a common exam trap is swapping steps 2 and 4.
Q7HardSORT vs DeepSORT
What specifically does DeepSORT add on top of SORT, and what problem does it solve?
  • AA Kalman filter — SORT had no motion model at all
  • BSelective Search region proposals
  • CA learned appearance re-identification embedding, cutting identity switches during brief occlusion
  • DReplacing IoU matching with pixel-level optical flow
SORT already runs a Kalman filter (motion-only matching via IoU); DeepSORT adds a second similarity cue — a learned appearance embedding — specifically to survive brief occlusions where motion alone loses the track. A is false, SORT already has a Kalman filter. B, D describe unrelated techniques.Common mistakeAssuming DeepSORT replaces the Kalman filter rather than augmenting it — it's still a hybrid, motion + appearance.
Q8HardROC northwest rule
Classifier A sits at (FPR=0.1, TPR=0.6); Classifier B sits at (FPR=0.3, TPR=0.6) on the same ROC plot. Which is better, and why?
  • AA — same TPR as B, but a lower FPR (fewer false alarms)
  • BB — a higher FPR means it's more willing to flag positives
  • CThey're equal — TPR is the only metric that matters
  • DCannot compare without knowing AUC
The northwest rule: same or better TPR at a lower FPR is strictly better — A sits up-and-left of B. B gets the direction backwards; a higher FPR is worse, not a virtue. C ignores FPR entirely. D is unnecessary — a direct northwest comparison between two specific points doesn't require the full-curve AUC.Common mistakeComparing only TPR (or only FPR) and ignoring that ROC evaluates the trade-off between both.
Q9HardAUC meaning
A classifier has AUC = 0.85. What does this number literally mean?
  • A85% of predictions are correct
  • B85% probability the classifier ranks a random positive instance above a random negative instance
  • C85% of the ROC curve lies above the diagonal
  • DThe classifier's precision is 0.85 at every threshold
AUC = P(score(random positive) > score(random negative)) — the Mann-Whitney U interpretation (Hanley & McNeil, 1982). A confuses AUC with accuracy, a threshold-dependent metric AUC deliberately avoids. C and D aren't what AUC measures at all.Common mistakeTreating AUC as "average accuracy" — it's a ranking-quality probability, independent of any single threshold.
Q10HardKalman noise trust
If measurement noise R is very large relative to process noise Q, what happens to the Kalman gain K, and what does that mean practically?
  • AK → 1; the filter ignores the motion model entirely
  • BK stays constant; R and Q don't affect gain, only covariance
  • CK → 0; the filter trusts its motion-model prediction over the noisy measurement
  • DK → ∞; the filter becomes unstable
Large R inflates the denominator of K = PpHT(HPpHT+R)−1, shrinking K toward 0 — the update term K·(z−Hxp) shrinks, so the corrected estimate stays close to the prediction. A and D describe the opposite regime (small R, or R→0). B is false — K is a direct function of both Q (via Pp) and R.Common mistakeMixing up which noise term should be large to trust which source — trace it through the K formula rather than memorizing the conclusion.
