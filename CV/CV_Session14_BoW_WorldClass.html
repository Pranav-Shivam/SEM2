How to read this guide

Session 14 sits at a hinge point in the course: it's the last major "classical" representation before deep learning fully takes over the pipeline, and it's also where the course explicitly zooms out to ask a bigger question — how should a vision system represent what it knows about the world? Bag of Words is the concrete, buildable answer for image classification; semantic hierarchies, part-whole models, attributes, syntactic grammars, and scene labelling are complementary answers to the same underlying question, each patching a different blind spot that plain BoW leaves open.

This guide follows that structure: first the BoW pipeline end-to-end (with the k-means codebook, quantization, spatial pyramid, and classification steps each getting their own section and, where useful, a live interactive demo), then the knowledge-representation ideas that sit alongside it, then a real diagnostic application, then a direct architectural contrast with deep learning, and finally synthesis and a 10-question exam quiz.

01

The Big Idea — Images as Orderless Documents

Core Concept

Intuition first — steal the trick from text retrieval

In text retrieval, a "bag of words" model represents a document purely by which words appear and how often — grammar, word order, and sentence structure are all thrown away. Two documents with identical word-count histograms are treated as equally relevant to a query, even if one is a coherent paragraph and the other is the same words shuffled into nonsense. That's a deliberate simplification: it's crude, but it's cheap, robust, and works astonishingly well for tasks like document classification and search.

Bag of Words for images makes exactly the same bet. Instead of asking "where exactly does this pattern occur, and how is it arranged relative to everything else," BoW asks only "what kinds of local visual stuff appeared in this image, and how much of each." An image becomes a histogram over a fixed vocabulary of "visual words," and two images with similar histograms are treated as visually similar — regardless of whether the underlying objects are in the same place, at the same scale, or in the same orientation.

What exactly is a "visual word"?

A visual word is not a real English word, and it isn't hand-defined by a person either. It's a cluster center discovered automatically by running k-means over a large pool of local image descriptors (e.g., SIFT descriptors) collected from training images. Each cluster center is a stand-in for a repeatable, recognizable local visual pattern — a corner, a blob of fur texture, an edge junction, a patch of brick — that tends to recur across many images. The full set of K cluster centers is called the codebook or visual vocabulary.

Why This Matters — the invariance/geometry trade you're always making

Rigid, exact-position matching is brittle: rotate a cat, occlude it behind a chair leg, or photograph it slightly zoomed in, and pixel-exact template matching fails immediately. Pooling local evidence into one orderless histogram buys BoW translation, rotation, and partial-occlusion invariance almost for free — an object can move around the frame, and as long as its local visual words still show up somewhere, the histogram barely changes.

The cost is exactly symmetric: BoW cannot say where in the image anything is, or how parts relate to each other spatially. A photo of "sky above grass" and a (physically nonsensical) composite of "grass above sky" made from the same patches produce the identical histogram. Every other idea in this session — spatial pyramids, part-whole models, syntactic grammars, scene labelling — exists specifically to claw back some of that discarded structure without giving up BoW's invariance benefits entirely.

Research Enhanced

Visual words were introduced for image/video retrieval by Sivic & Zisserman's "Video Google" (ICCV 2003), and formalized as a full classification pipeline (codebook + histogram + SVM) by Csurka, Dance, Fan, Willamowski & Bray, "Visual Categorization with Bags of Keypoints" (ECCV Workshop on Statistical Learning in Computer Vision, 2004) — still the most commonly cited "origin paper" for BoW image classification. Both predate deep learning by roughly a decade and were, for several years, the dominant paradigm for image classification benchmarks like Caltech-101.

02

Step 1-2 — Detect & Describe, Codebook Construction

Pipeline Steps 1-2

Step 1 — Detect & Describe

The pipeline starts by extracting a set of local descriptors from every training image, using one of three families of methods:

  1. SIFT (Scale-Invariant Feature Transform)

    Finds "interesting," repeatable keypoints via a Difference-of-Gaussians scale-space, then describes each as a 128-d gradient-orientation histogram. Scale- and rotation-invariant by construction — the classic choice when descriptors must survive zoom and in-plane rotation.

  2. Harris corners

    Detects corner-like structure using the eigenvalues of the second-moment (structure) matrix — points where intensity changes sharply in two directions at once. Good at localizing distinctive junctions, but not inherently scale-invariant on its own.

  3. Dense grid sampling

    Skips "interestingness" detection entirely: place a descriptor (often still SIFT-style) at every point on a regular grid, regardless of whether that patch looks distinctive. Guarantees uniform coverage of low-gradient, texture-heavy regions (sky, grass, skin) that a keypoint detector would simply skip — a major reason dense sampling is preferred for whole-image classification tasks rather than sparse keypoint matching.

Step 2 — Codebook Construction via k-means

Pool the local descriptors from a large sample of training images (potentially millions of descriptor vectors) and cluster them with k-means. Each of the resulting K cluster centers becomes one visual word; together they form the codebook.

  1. Initialize

    Pick K initial cluster centers (commonly at random, or via a smarter seeding scheme like k-means++).

  2. Assign

    Assign every pooled descriptor to its nearest center by Euclidean distance.

  3. Update

    Recompute each center as the mean of all descriptors currently assigned to it.

  4. Repeat

    Iterate assign/update until the centers stop moving (convergence) or a max iteration count is reached.

k-means objective being minimized
J = Σk=1..K Σd ∈ cluster k ‖ d − ck ‖² d = a local descriptor vector, ck = the k-th cluster center (visual word) K is a hyperparameter — typical range: K ≈ 200 – 5000

Exam Radar

Codebook size K — exam radar

  • K is a bias-variance dial: too small → visual words are too generic, histograms lose the power to tell classes apart (underfitting). Too large → each word is over-specific, histograms become sparse and noise-sensitive, and generalization suffers (overfitting), on top of much slower k-means and quantization.
  • There is no closed-form "optimal K" — it's selected empirically via cross-validation, exactly like choosing the number of hidden units or trees in any other model.
  • Common mistake: assuming "bigger codebook = strictly better." It trades discriminability for robustness and compute — both directions have a real cost.
03

Step 3-4 — Quantization & Histogram Encoding

Pipeline Steps 3-4

Step 3 — Quantization: turning descriptors into word IDs

Every local descriptor extracted from a new image is assigned to its nearest visual word in the codebook — this is vector quantization, exactly the "assign" step of k-means but now applied at test/encoding time against the fixed, already-trained codebook.

w(d) = argmink ‖ d − ck ‖ — hard assignment: descriptor d is quantized to its single nearest codeword

Two refinements soften this hard, winner-take-all commitment:

Soft assignment distributes a descriptor's weight across several nearby cluster centers (e.g., via a kernel weighted by distance) instead of committing 100% to the single nearest one. This reduces quantization error for descriptors that fall near a boundary between two clusters, where a hard assignment is essentially an arbitrary coin-flip.

VLAD (Vector of Locally Aggregated Descriptors) goes further: instead of just counting how many descriptors land in each cluster, it accumulates the residual vectors (d − ck) for all descriptors assigned to cluster k. This encodes first-order information about how descriptors differ from their assigned center, not just how many there are — producing a much higher-dimensional, more discriminative encoding for the same K.

Fisher Vectors generalize this further still: fit a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) over the descriptor space instead of hard k-means clusters, then encode an image by the gradient of the log-likelihood with respect to the GMM's parameters (mixture weights, means, variances). This captures both first- and second-order statistics and was, for a period just before deep CNN features took over, the state of the art for large-scale image classification.

Step 4 — Histogram Encoding & Normalization

Once every descriptor in an image has a word ID, build a K-bin histogram counting how many descriptors were quantized to each visual word. Raw counts, however, are scale-dependent (a bigger image with more descriptors just has bigger counts everywhere) — so the histogram is always normalized before being fed to a classifier.

L1 normalization: hk ← hk / Σj hj — turns the histogram into a proper probability distribution; pairs naturally with a histogram-intersection or χ² kernel L2 normalization: hk ← hk / ‖h‖₂ — unit-length vector; the natural choice for cosine similarity / linear and RBF-kernel SVMs, and standard practice for high-dimensional VLAD/Fisher vectors

04

Spatial Pyramid Matching — A Cheap Semantic Hierarchy of Layout

Pipeline Step 4 (optional enhancement)

Intuition first — reintroducing "where," cheaply

Plain BoW is so aggressively orderless that it literally cannot distinguish a photo of "sky above grass" from an (impossible) composite of "grass above sky" made of the same patches — both produce the identical histogram. Spatial Pyramid Matching (SPM) fixes this without giving up BoW's invariance benefits wholesale: instead of computing one histogram over the whole image, compute a separate K-bin histogram inside each cell of an increasingly fine grid, then concatenate all of those cell-histograms into one long feature vector.

  1. Level 0 — 1×1 grid (the whole image)

    One K-bin histogram over the entire image — this is exactly plain BoW.

  2. Level 1 — 2×2 grid (4 cells)

    Split the image into quadrants; compute an independent K-bin histogram inside each quadrant.

  3. Level 2 — 4×4 grid (16 cells)

    Split further into a 4×4 grid of 16 cells; compute an independent K-bin histogram inside each.

  4. Concatenate

    Stack every cell's histogram, from every level, into one feature vector. The final descriptor now carries coarse positional information — which quadrant/cell each visual word tended to occur in — while every individual cell's histogram is still fully orderless internally.

Final feature dimensionality
Cells per level: 1×1 → 1 cell, 2×2 → 4 cells, 4×4 → 16 cells Total cells across all 3 levels: 1 + 4 + 16 = 21 Final dimensionality: K × 21 (e.g. K=200 → a 4200-dim feature vector)

⊞ Interactive · Toggle Pyramid Levels

Research Enhanced

Spatial Pyramid Matching is due to Lazebnik, Schmid & Ponce, "Beyond Bags of Features: Spatial Pyramid Matching for Recognizing Natural Scene Categories" (CVPR 2006). Their pyramid match kernel also weights each level unevenly — coarser levels get weight 1/2L and finer levels get weight 1/2(L−l+1) (for a 3-level pyramid: weights 1/4, 1/4, 1/2 for levels 0, 1, 2 respectively) — because a match found at a fine cell necessarily implies a match at every coarser cell containing it, so naive summation would double-count fine-level agreement unless it's reweighted.

Key Insight

Notice the structural echo with Section 06's semantic hierarchy: both ideas fight the "too orderless / too flat" problem with the same trick — a coarse-to-fine, multi-resolution structure. SPM applies it to spatial layout (1×1 → 2×2 → 4×4); semantic hierarchies apply the identical coarse-to-fine idea to category structure (animal → dog → poodle). Different axis, same design pattern.

05

Step 5 — Classification

Pipeline Step 5

From histogram to label

Once every training image is reduced to a fixed-length vector — a K-dim histogram, or a K×21-dim spatial-pyramid feature — image classification collapses into ordinary vector classification. Three standard choices:

  1. SVM

    The most common choice historically (used by both Csurka et al. 2004 and Lazebnik et al. 2006). Because plain dot-product similarity is a poor match for comparing two histograms/distributions, BoW-SVM pipelines typically use a histogram-intersection or χ² (chi-squared) kernel instead of a linear kernel, both of which are specifically designed to compare distributions.

  2. k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN)

    A simple, non-parametric baseline: compare a query histogram against all training histograms (again typically via χ² or Euclidean distance) and take a majority vote among the k closest.

  3. Linear classifiers

    Cheap and scale gracefully to very high-dimensional features — especially important once you move to VLAD, Fisher Vectors, or a spatial-pyramid feature with tens of thousands of dimensions. L2-normalized VLAD/Fisher encodings are specifically engineered to work well with a plain linear SVM, side-stepping the need for an expensive non-linear kernel altogether.

Exam Radar

Classifier choice — exam radar

  • Codebook size K and classifier choice are two separate but coupled hyperparameters — a larger K produces sparser, higher-dimensional histograms that tend to favor linear classifiers over kernel SVMs (compute cost of a non-linear kernel grows with feature dimension and training-set size).
  • Know why a linear/dot-product kernel is a poor default for raw histogram counts: it doesn't respect the fact that histograms are distributions, not arbitrary vectors — hence χ²/intersection kernels, or L2-normalizing first.
06

Knowledge Representation Mechanisms

Beyond the Histogram

Intuition first — a histogram is a "flat," structure-blind representation

A BoW/SPM histogram treats every visual word as an independent count — it has no built-in notion that a poodle is a kind of dog, no notion that legs must attach to a torso in a physically constrained way, and no way to transfer what it has learned about one class to help recognize a related but unseen class. Three complementary knowledge-representation mechanisms patch different pieces of this gap.

1. Semantic Hierarchy / Ontology

An explicit class hierarchy — e.g. poodle ⊂ dog ⊂ animal — organized as a tree or DAG (WordNet-style; this is literally how ImageNet's thousands of categories are structured). Two concrete payoffs:

Transfer learning: a classifier that has learned strong general "dog" features from thousands of labeled dog photos can bootstrap recognition of a rarer sub-class like "poodle" using far fewer poodle-specific examples, because poodle inherits everything the model already knows about dog.

Evidence propagation: strong visual evidence for "dog" in a region should raise belief in the parent class "animal" and lower belief in unrelated branches ("vehicle," "furniture") — reasoning that flows up and down the tree, rather than the 1,000+ leaf classes being treated as fully independent, mutually exclusive hypotheses.

2. Part-Whole Relations

Objects are represented as a set of parts plus a model of their relative spatial arrangement — exactly the structure a plain BoW histogram cannot express, since it only counts word occurrences and never their relative positions.

Constellation models (Fergus, Perona & Zisserman, "Object Class Recognition by Unsupervised Scale-Invariant Learning," CVPR 2003) represent an object class as a small number of parts, each with its own appearance model, plus a joint probabilistic model over their relative positions and scales.

Pictorial structures (Felzenszwalb & Huttenlocher, "Pictorial Structures for Object Recognition," IJCV 2005) model an object as parts connected by "springs" — a cost function that penalizes implausible deformations away from a rest configuration. This is the direct conceptual ancestor of the Deformable Parts Model (DPM) used as a detector baseline elsewhere in the course.

3. Attributes

Human-nameable, mid-level properties — has-stripes, is-furry, has-wheels — that cut across the class hierarchy rather than sitting inside it. Because attributes are shared across many classes, a model that has learned "has-stripes" from zebras and tigers can recognize a novel striped animal it has never been directly trained on — the classic zero-shot learning setup, formalized in Lampert, Nickisch & Harmeling's Direct/Indirect Attribute Prediction (DAP/IAP) framework, "Learning to Detect Unseen Object Classes by Between-Class Attribute Transfer" (CVPR 2009).

Why This Matters — generalization to rare or unseen categories

All three mechanisms share one motivation: real-world category distributions are heavy-tailed — a handful of common classes have abundant labeled data, while most classes have very little. Hierarchies, part-sharing, and attribute-sharing are all ways of letting a model exploit structure between classes so that data-rich classes can help data-poor or entirely unseen ones, instead of every class needing to be learned from scratch in isolation.

07

Syntactic Pattern Recognition & Scene Grammars

Complementary Approach

Intuition first — objects as sentences

Syntactic (structural) pattern recognition treats an object — or an entire scene — the way a linguist treats a sentence: built from primitive parts ("terminals," analogous to words) combined according to production rules ("grammar," analogous to syntax). This is a fundamentally different move from BoW's "count what's present" strategy: a grammar doesn't just ask which parts are present, it demands they appear in a legal arrangement.

The canonical example: a bicycle isn't simply "wheel-ish blob + wheel-ish blob + frame-ish blob" scattered anywhere in the image (which is roughly what a bag-of-parts count would look for) — it's specifically two wheels and a frame in one particular, legal spatial arrangement (both wheels below the frame, roughly parallel to each other, the frame connecting their axles). The grammar's production rules encode exactly which arrangements are legal and reject everything else, including plausible-looking-but-wrong configurations (e.g., one wheel stacked directly on top of the other).

Scene grammars — And-Or graphs

This idea scales from single objects up to entire scenes via scene grammars, most notably formalized as And-Or graphs in Zhu & Mumford's "A Stochastic Grammar of Images" (Foundations and Trends in Computer Graphics and Vision, 2006):

  1. "And" node

    Says a scene or object decomposes into all of its children simultaneously — e.g., bicycle = wheel AND wheel AND frame. Every child must be present and correctly arranged for the parent to be valid.

  2. "Or" node

    Says a part can be realized in any one of several alternative ways — e.g., wheel = spoked-wheel OR solid-disc-wheel. This is how the grammar captures legitimate visual variation without treating every variant as an unrelated, separately-learned category.

  3. Parsing

    Interpreting an image means finding the most probable derivation tree through the And-Or graph that explains the observed image — recursively enforcing legality at every level of the compositional hierarchy, from whole scene down to primitive part.

Exam Radar

BoW vs syntactic grammars — exam radar

  • BoW's orderless histogram has literally no mechanism to reject an illegal spatial arrangement of correct parts — a syntactic grammar's entire purpose is enforcing exactly that kind of global structural legality.
  • Know the And/Or distinction precisely: And = all children required (compositional necessity); Or = any one alternative suffices (legitimate variation).
  • Common mistake: confusing a scene grammar's "legal arrangement" constraint with a constellation model's "relative position" model (Section 06) — they're closely related in spirit but the grammar formalism is explicitly recursive/hierarchical (parts have sub-parts, scenes have objects have parts), while a constellation model is typically a single flat layer of parts.

Scene Labelling — Appearance Meets Context

Complementary Approach

Intuition first — labelling every region, not just the whole image

Scene labelling (also called scene parsing or semantic labelling) assigns a class label to every region of an image jointly — historically, every superpixel or fixed-size patch; today, every single pixel — rather than producing one label for the whole image the way a BoW classifier does. It fuses two distinct sources of evidence:

Local appearance: what does this specific region look like on its own? (e.g., a BoW-style visual-word histogram computed just within that region, feeding a per-region classifier.)

Contextual compatibility: how consistent is this region's label with its neighbors' labels, given real-world regularities? (e.g., "sky sits above road," "road sits below car," "a keyboard sits on a desk, not floating on a ceiling.")

Historical implementation — MRFs and CRFs

This joint reasoning was classically implemented with probabilistic graphical models: Markov Random Fields (MRFs) or Conditional Random Fields (CRFs), combining a unary term (per-region appearance likelihood, e.g. from a BoW-based per-region classifier) with a pairwise term (a learned or hand-designed compatibility cost between the labels of adjacent regions), solved via approximate inference such as graph cuts or belief propagation. Well-known examples include TextonBoost (Shotton, Winn, Rother & Criminisi, ECCV 2006) and multiscale CRFs for image labeling (He, Zemel & Carreira-Perpiñán, CVPR 2004).

Modern implementation — Fully Convolutional Networks

Modern approaches replace the explicit two-part appearance + hand-designed-context pipeline with Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs) (Long, Shelhamer & Darrell, "Fully Convolutional Networks for Semantic Segmentation," CVPR 2015): a single end-to-end network implicitly learns both local appearance and contextual regularities together — through its receptive field size and the statistics baked into training data — and directly outputs a dense per-pixel label map, with no separate hand-built CRF stage required.

Research Enhanced

The MRF/CRF idea didn't vanish once FCNs arrived — it was partially re-attached on top. DeepLab v1/v2 (Chen, Papandreou, Kokkinos, Murphy & Yuille, 2014/2016) famously bolted a fully-connected dense CRF onto an FCN's output as a post-processing refinement step, sharpening object boundaries that the FCN's coarse feature maps otherwise blur. This shows the "combine local appearance with contextual compatibility" principle from classical scene labelling persisting well into the deep learning era, just implemented in a hybrid rather than purely hand-designed form.

Medical Application — Breast Tumour Patches

Applied BoW

The pipeline: sliding-window patch classification

A whole-slide histopathology image is far too large — often gigapixel-scale — to classify as a single unit. Instead, a window is slid across the slide, and each window (patch) is independently encoded as a BoW-style histogram over local texture descriptors and classified as tumour or normal tissue.

Why BoW specifically helps here

Histopathology slides show heavy stain-color variation across labs, staining batches, and even individual scanner calibration — H&E-stained tissue can look meaningfully different in raw RGB from one hospital's slides to another's, purely as an artifact of the staining and imaging process rather than any real biological difference. A classifier trained directly on raw RGB pixel statistics is highly sensitive to exactly this kind of domain shift.

A BoW codebook built over local texture descriptors instead captures stable, largely stain-invariant local structure — nuclei density and packing, gland architecture, chromatin texture — because these gradient- or local-intensity-pattern-based descriptors substantially factor out absolute color. The same underlying tissue architecture produces similar visual-word statistics whether the slide happens to be stained a bit more purple or a bit more pink.

From patch to slide: aggregation

Evaluation aggregates the many per-patch predictions across an entire slide into one slide-level verdict, typically via:

Majority voting: count what fraction of patches are classified tumour-positive and threshold that fraction to reach a whole-slide decision.

Heatmaps: render a spatial map of per-patch tumour probability overlaid on the slide — clinically valuable in its own right, since it shows a pathologist where the suspicious tissue is located, not just a single global yes/no.

Exam Radar

Medical BoW — exam radar

  • Know precisely why texture-based BoW beats raw RGB here — stain-color robustness, not just "texture is generally better."
  • Know the two-stage structure: a patch-level BoW classifier first, then a separate slide-level aggregation step (majority vote or heatmap) on top — this two-stage pattern generalizes to almost any "image too big to classify whole" problem.

BoW vs Deep Learning — A Fundamental Architectural Difference

Synthesis

Two different answers to "how much geometry should we keep?"

BoW discards exact geometry by design. At the histogram-pooling step, it deliberately throws away where within the image (or within a spatial-pyramid cell) any given visual word occurred — keeping only what kinds of local patterns are present and how many. Spatial pyramids only coarsen this trade-off; they never fully undo it.

CNNs learn geometric invariances hierarchically, through architecture, rather than discarding geometry by a single fixed design choice. Early convolutional layers detect local, position-tied patterns (edges, corners) while still preserving a spatially-organized feature map. Successive convolution + pooling stages progressively build translation- and scale-tolerant, increasingly abstract and semantic features — but the network decides, end-to-end from data, exactly which invariances are worth learning and at what stage, rather than committing to "discard all position information" in one irreversible step the way BoW's histogram pooling does. Only at the very final classification stage (global pooling / fully-connected layers) does a CNN give up fine spatial structure entirely.

Why This Matters — this is architecture, not just a performance gap

It's tempting to describe the BoW → CNN transition purely in terms of accuracy numbers (CNNs simply score higher), but the more important distinction for understanding why is architectural: BoW is orderless by an explicit, hand-designed choice made once, at a fixed point in the pipeline. A CNN is never orderless by design — it retains spatial structure throughout most of its depth and only sacrifices it, gradually and adaptively, exactly where the training data says that sacrifice pays off.

Key Insight

Section 04's spatial pyramid and a CNN's convolutional feature maps are solving the exact same underlying problem — "plain orderless pooling throws away too much" — via two very different mechanisms: one hand-designed and fixed (the pyramid grid, chosen before training), one learned end-to-end (the convolution + pooling hierarchy, shaped by training data itself).

Conceptual Deep Dives

Compare & Contrast
BoW and syntactic pattern recognition both decompose an image into local pieces — so why are they considered fundamentally different representations, not just two flavors of the same idea?

  1. BoW's representation is a flat multiset: a histogram only records how many of each visual word occurred. It has no concept of "arrangement" at all — every permutation of the same parts produces the identical representation.

  2. A syntactic grammar's representation is a derivation tree: it records not just which parts (terminals) are present, but which production rules combined them, which directly encodes legal spatial/compositional arrangement and rejects illegal ones.

  3. So the difference isn't "more parts vs fewer parts" — it's "unordered multiset" vs "structured derivation," a difference in representational power, not just in accuracy.

Key Insight

Any two representations that agree on "which parts are present" can still disagree completely on whether a given image is valid — arrangement information is exactly what separates them.

Spatial Pyramid Matching adds position information back into BoW — does that mean SPM secretly abandons the "orderless" assumption?

  1. SPM only adds coarse, fixed-grid position — which of a small, predetermined set of cells (1, 4, or 16) a descriptor fell into. It says nothing about position within a cell, and nothing about the relative position of two visual words inside the same cell.

  2. Every individual cell's histogram is still fully orderless internally — SPM stacks multiple orderless histograms at different spatial granularities, it doesn't replace orderlessness with a real geometric model.

  3. So SPM doesn't abandon the orderless assumption — it coarsens the resolution at which the assumption is applied, trading away a little bit of invariance (an object slightly shifted might now land in a different cell) for a little bit of positional discriminability.

Key Insight

"Adding position information" and "abandoning orderlessness" are not the same move — SPM is squarely in between plain BoW and a true part-whole geometric model like a constellation model.

Why can't you just pick the largest codebook size K your compute budget allows and call it done?

  1. A larger K makes each visual word more specific — narrower clusters, capturing finer distinctions between local patterns. That sounds strictly better for discriminability.

  2. But narrower clusters are also less robust: small amounts of image noise, lighting change, or viewpoint shift can now push a descriptor into a neighboring, "wrong" cluster more easily, since cluster boundaries are packed tighter together. Histograms also get sparser (most bins near-zero for any one image), which hurts distance-based classifiers and encourages overfitting to training-set-specific noise patterns.

  3. This is exactly a bias-variance/model-capacity trade-off, the same shape of problem as choosing the number of hidden units in a network or the depth of a decision tree — more capacity is not free, and the right amount is found empirically via validation, not by maximizing compute spend.

Key Insight

"More capacity always helps" is one of the most common wrong instincts in ML — K is a textbook case where it visibly doesn't.

C and D both try to quantize before the codebook exists, which is impossible — quantization requires the codebook's cluster centers as a reference.Common mistakeForgetting that codebook construction (k-means, done once over pooled training descriptors) is a separate, earlier step from quantization (done per-image, per-descriptor, against that fixed codebook).
Q2EasyVisual words
What exactly is a "visual word"?
  • AA single raw pixel value
  • BA hand-labeled semantic category like "car" or "dog"
  • CA k-means cluster center in the space of local image descriptors
  • DA bounding box drawn by an annotator
A visual word is a codebook entry — one of the K cluster centers discovered automatically by k-means over pooled local descriptors (e.g. SIFT vectors), standing in for a recurring local visual pattern. A confuses it with raw pixel intensity. B confuses it with the final class label BoW is trying to predict. D confuses it with detection/localization annotation, unrelated to codebook construction.Common mistakeAssuming visual words are semantically meaningful or human-interpretable by design — they're purely a statistical clustering artifact, though they sometimes happen to correspond to interpretable patterns like "corner" or "fur texture."
Q3MediumSampling strategy
For whole-image classification (as opposed to sparse keypoint matching), why is dense grid sampling often preferred over a keypoint detector like Harris or DoG (SIFT's detector)?
  • ADense sampling is always faster to compute than keypoint detection
  • BKeypoint detectors skip low-gradient, texture-heavy regions (sky, grass) that still carry useful classification signal; dense sampling guarantees uniform coverage
  • CDense sampling is scale-invariant while keypoint detectors are not
  • DKeypoint detectors cannot produce SIFT-style descriptors
A keypoint detector deliberately fires only at "interesting," high-gradient locations — it will systematically skip smooth regions like sky or grass, even though those regions' textures can still be informative for classifying the whole scene. Dense sampling ignores "interestingness" and places a descriptor everywhere on a regular grid. A is not generally true (dense sampling usually produces far MORE descriptors, costing more compute downstream). C is backwards — SIFT's own detector is explicitly scale-invariant; dense sampling at one grid isn't. D is false — dense sampling commonly still uses a SIFT-style descriptor, just without SIFT's own keypoint detector.Common mistakeAssuming "detector-based is always better because it's smarter" — for classification (not matching/retrieval), uniform coverage often matters more than picking only the most distinctive points.
Q4MediumNormalization
Why must a raw visual-word count histogram be normalized (L1 or L2) before being used for classification?
  • ARaw counts scale with the number of descriptors extracted from an image, so an unnormalized histogram would make two visually similar images of different sizes/descriptor-densities look artificially different
  • BNormalization is required to make k-means converge
  • COnly normalized histograms can be visualized as bar charts
  • DNormalization converts the histogram into a spatial pyramid
Raw bin counts depend on how many total descriptors an image produced — a bigger image, or one sampled more densely, will have larger counts everywhere even if the underlying visual content is identical in proportion. Normalizing (L1: sum to 1; L2: unit length) removes this scale-dependence. B confuses histogram normalization (an encoding step) with k-means convergence (a separate, earlier clustering step). C and D are both fabricated, unrelated claims.Common mistakeThinking L1 and L2 normalization are interchangeable — they pair with different downstream comparisons (L1 with histogram-intersection/χ² kernels, L2 with cosine similarity/linear kernels).
Q5HardSpatial pyramid dims
Using a codebook of K=200 and a 3-level spatial pyramid (1×1, 2×2, 4×4), what is the final feature vector's dimensionality?
  • A200
  • B800
  • C4200
  • D3200
Total cells across all 3 levels = 1 (1×1) + 4 (2×2) + 16 (4×4) = 21. Final dimensionality = K × 21 = 200 × 21 = 4200. A (200) is just plain BoW with no pyramid (level 0 only). B (800) mistakenly uses only 4 total cells (200×4). D (3200) mistakenly uses only 16 cells (200×16), forgetting to include levels 0 and 1.Common mistakeForgetting to sum cells across ALL levels (1+4+16=21) — a common slip is using only the finest level's cell count.
Q6MediumSemantic hierarchy
A model trained mostly on generic "dog" images can recognize a rare sub-breed like "poodle" with only a handful of poodle-specific examples. Which knowledge-representation mechanism explains this most directly?
  • ASyntactic pattern recognition
  • BScene labelling via CRF
  • CSpatial pyramid matching
  • DSemantic hierarchy / ontology, via transfer learning down the class tree
This is exactly the transfer-learning payoff of a semantic hierarchy: since poodle ⊂ dog, features and evidence learned for the broad "dog" class transfer directly to the rare "poodle" leaf class. A concerns legal spatial arrangement of parts, not category inheritance. B concerns per-region contextual labelling in a scene, unrelated to class hierarchy. C concerns spatial layout of visual words within an image, not category relationships.Common mistakeConfusing "attributes" (shared properties across classes, enabling zero-shot transfer to classes with NO training examples) with "semantic hierarchy" (an explicit parent-child structure enabling transfer to classes with FEW training examples) — both aid generalization, but via different mechanisms.
Q7HardAnd-Or graphs
In a scene grammar's And-Or graph, what does an "Or" node represent?
  • AAll child parts are required simultaneously for the parent to be valid
  • BAny one of several alternative realizations of a part is acceptable
  • CA pairwise compatibility cost between neighboring image regions
  • DThe k-means cluster assignment of a descriptor
An "Or" node captures legitimate visual variation — e.g., wheel = spoked-wheel OR solid-disc-wheel — any one alternative satisfies that part of the grammar. A describes an "And" node instead (all children required — e.g., bicycle = wheel AND wheel AND frame). C describes the pairwise term in an MRF/CRF for scene labelling, a different mechanism entirely. D describes vector quantization in the BoW pipeline, unrelated to grammars.Common mistakeMixing up And/Or with the unrelated MRF/CRF unary/pairwise terms from scene labelling — both are "combine multiple pieces of evidence" ideas but from different formalisms answering different questions (legality of structure vs. joint per-region labelling).
Q8MediumScene labelling
What is the key architectural difference between classical MRF/CRF-based scene labelling and a modern Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) approach?
  • AMRF/CRF explicitly separates a unary appearance term and a pairwise context term, solved via approximate inference; an FCN learns appearance and context jointly, end-to-end, in one network
  • BMRF/CRF operates on whole images only, while FCNs operate on regions only
  • CFCNs cannot produce per-pixel output, only per-image labels
  • DMRF/CRF requires no training data, while FCNs require large labeled datasets
Classical scene labelling hand-designs two separate terms (unary appearance + pairwise context) and combines them via graph-based inference; an FCN instead learns a single end-to-end mapping from image to dense per-pixel labels, implicitly capturing both appearance and context through its receptive field and training data. B reverses the actual region/pixel granularities involved. C is false — FCNs are specifically designed for dense per-pixel output. D is false — MRF/CRF unary and pairwise terms are typically also learned from labeled training data.Common mistakeAssuming CRF post-processing (e.g. DeepLab's dense CRF on top of FCN output) means the two approaches are mutually exclusive — modern pipelines have combined them.
Q9MediumMedical BoW
Why does a BoW texture-based codebook outperform raw RGB pixel classification for breast tumour histopathology patches?
  • ARGB images cannot be processed by k-means
  • BBoW histograms are always higher resolution than raw pixels
  • CLocal texture descriptors (nuclei density, gland architecture) largely factor out absolute color, making BoW more robust to stain-color variation across labs/scanners than raw RGB statistics
  • DBoW requires no training data, unlike RGB-based classifiers
Histopathology slides vary heavily in stain color across labs, batches, and scanners — a purely color/RGB-based classifier is highly sensitive to this domain shift. Local texture/gradient-based descriptors quantized into a codebook capture structural patterns (nuclei packing, gland shape) that are much more stable across this color variation. A is false — k-means and descriptor extraction operate on RGB (or grayscale-derived) images just fine. B is a fabricated, irrelevant claim about resolution. D is false — the BoW classifier (SVM/kNN/etc.) is trained exactly like any other supervised model.Common mistakeAnswering generically ("texture is more informative than color") instead of the specific, exam-relevant reason: stain-color domain shift robustness.
Q10HardBoW vs CNN
What is the most precise way to describe the difference between how BoW and CNNs handle geometric/spatial information?
  • ACNNs simply achieve higher accuracy than BoW; there is no meaningful architectural difference in how they represent geometry
  • BBoW discards exact spatial position by a fixed, one-time design choice at the histogram-pooling step; CNNs preserve spatial structure through most of their depth and learn, end-to-end, which invariances to build and when
  • CBoW preserves more spatial information than CNNs because of spatial pyramid matching
  • DCNNs use k-means clustering internally to discard geometry, just like BoW
The BoW vs CNN distinction is architectural, not just a score difference: BoW makes one hand-designed, irreversible choice to pool away position at the histogram step (SPM only coarsens this, never removes it); a CNN's convolutional feature maps retain spatial layout across most of the network's depth, and the network learns from data exactly which invariances (translation, scale, etc.) to build and at which layer. A wrongly reduces an architectural difference to a pure accuracy gap. C is backwards — even with SPM, BoW still discards far more spatial detail than a CNN's feature maps retain. D is false — CNNs do not use k-means; their spatial handling comes from learned convolutional filters and pooling.Common mistakeDescribing the BoW→CNN transition only in terms of "CNNs are more accurate" without being able to explain the underlying architectural/representational reason why.
