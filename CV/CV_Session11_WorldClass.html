01

Segmentation Taxonomy

Slides 2–3

What segmentation actually asks

A digital photo is just a grid of numbers — the computer has no built-in notion of "cat" or "grass." Image segmentation is the process of sorting pixels into meaningful groups, like filling in a paint-by-numbers picture: every pixel belonging to the same object or region gets grouped together (Slide 2). The question it answers is narrower and harder than it sounds: not "what's in this picture?" and not "roughly where is it?", but "which exact pixels make up this thing?"

The classic CS231n-style cat/dog panel (Slide 2) lines up four related-but-distinct vision tasks along a spectrum of increasing pixel-level precision:

TaskWhat it outputsKnows individual instances?
Classification + LocalizationOne label + one bounding box ("CAT")N/A — single object assumed
Object DetectionA bounding box + label per object ("DOG, DOG, CAT")Yes, but only a box, not an outline
Semantic SegmentationEvery pixel labeled by class (GRASS, CAT, TREE, SKY)No — "cat" pixels, not "cat #1" pixels
Instance SegmentationPixel-precise mask per object instanceYes — two dogs get two different-colored masks

Six flavors of segmentation (Slide 3)

Slide 3 widens the taxonomy to six categories that roughly track the field's historical evolution — from cheap classical image processing to modern learned, semantic-aware methods:

TypeCore ideaEra / typical method
ThresholdingPick a brightness cutoff — darker = object, lighter = backgroundClassical (Otsu's method, 1979)
Edge-basedFind boundaries via sharp intensity gradientsClassical (Sobel, Canny 1986)
Region-basedGrow a region outward from a seed pixel while neighbors stay similarClassical (region growing, watershed)
Semantic SegmentationLabel every pixel by class, no instance countDeep learning (FCN 2015, U-Net)
Instance SegmentationSemantic + separates individual objects of the same classDeep learning (Mask R-CNN, 2017)
Panoptic Segmentation"Stuff" (background classes) + "things" (countable instances), unifiedDeep learning (Panoptic FPN, 2019)
Key insight

Panoptic segmentation isn't "yet another type" — it's the merger that closes the gap semantic segmentation leaves open. It labels the uncountable background ("stuff": sky, road, grass) and separately masks each countable object ("things": each car, each person) in one unified task.

Source note

"More advanced" doesn't mean "always better" — thresholding remains the right, cheap, fast choice for simple controlled scenarios like barcode or document scanning (Slide 3, Researched Context). Real deployments pick the cheapest technique that meets the accuracy bar, not the fanciest one.

Researched

Panoptic segmentation was formalized by Kirillov et al. (2019, Facebook AI Research) specifically to unify per-pixel category labels ("stuff") with per-instance masks ("things") into one task and one metric — Panoptic Quality (PQ). Self-driving stacks need exactly this: semantic labels for drivable road/lane surface plus individually-tracked masks for every pedestrian and vehicle, in real time.

Kirillov et al. 2019; PyImageSearch, Ultralytics, Labellerr (via slide Researched Context)
Exam Alert

Semantic vs. instance vs. panoptic — the classic mix-up

  • Semantic segmentation labels what class a pixel is, but two adjacent objects of the same class merge into one blob — it cannot count "how many cats."
  • Instance segmentation adds object identity: same class, but each individual object gets its own mask (dog #1 vs. dog #2).
  • Panoptic segmentation = semantic (for "stuff": sky, road, grass — uncountable) + instance (for "things": cars, people — countable), scored with a single metric, Panoptic Quality (PQ).
  • Do not confuse instance segmentation with object detection — detection only draws a box; instance segmentation traces the exact pixel outline.
02

Gestalt Psychology & Perceptual Grouping

Slides 4–6

"The whole is different from the sum of its parts"

Before any lecture on algorithms, the course pauses on Gestalt psychology (Wertheimer, Köhler, Koffka — early-20th-century Germany), because it names the exact problem segmentation has to solve. Slide 4's claim: "we perceive objects in their entirety before their individual parts." Human vision doesn't build a dog out of edges one at a time — it locks onto the whole shape almost instantly, using both raw pixels (bottom-up) and prior knowledge (top-down).

Slide 4 — verbatim

"We perceive objects in its entirety before their individual parts"

The hidden Dalmatian: figure-ground segregation

Slide 4's speckled black-and-white image — the famous R. C. James Dalmatian photograph — looks like pure noise at first glance. The moment you're told "there's a dog sniffing the ground in there," your brain locks onto the whole dog shape and you can never unsee it again, even though not a single pixel changed. That flip is a textbook demonstration of top-down processing: prior knowledge changes what you consciously perceive from identical raw input, and of figure-ground segregation — separating an object ("figure") from its surrounding context ("ground").

This maps directly onto why segmentation is hard for machines: a model looking at raw pixel intensities has no built-in "aha" moment. Getting scattered, disconnected pixel patches recognized as one coherent "dog" is functionally the same challenge, minus the human shortcut.

Illusory contours, occlusion, familiar configuration

Slide 5 (referencing a Stanford CS231-style tutorial) stacks three more Gestalt cues that force the brain to see one unified whole out of separate, incomplete fragments:

  1. Illusory / subjective contours (Kanizsa triangle)

    Three "Pac-Man" disks with aligned wedge cutouts make the brain perceive a bright triangle floating on top — edges that physically don't exist. First described by Gaetano Kanizsa (1955); the illusory triangle is even perceived as brighter than the background.

  2. Occlusion

    Two blob shapes positioned so one appears to continue behind the other — the same principle that lets a self-driving car treat a pedestrian partly hidden behind a parked car as one continuous person, not two fragments.

  3. Familiar configuration

    A ring of disconnected spike shapes, or separate curved segments, snap into a recognizable whole once the pattern is known — meaningless in isolation, obvious together.

Researched

Kanizsa figures are still active research tools for studying "amodal completion" — including recent work testing whether CNNs and vision transformers reproduce the illusion. Most don't, without explicit training, exposing a real gap between human and machine perceptual grouping.

"Finding Closure: A Closer Look at the Gestalt Law of Closure in CNNs," arXiv 2408.12460 (via slide Researched Context)

The Gestalt grouping laws (Slide 6)

Slide 6 is the "cheat sheet" — the specific rules the brain uses to decide which separate marks belong together. Flip each card below for the one-line definition.

Proximity
Elements placed close together are perceived as one group, even if otherwise identical to elements spaced further apart.
Similarity
Elements that look alike (shape, color, orientation) are grouped together even when scattered across the scene.
Common Fate
Elements moving in the same direction are perceived as one group — a flock of birds veering together reads as "one flock."
Common Region
Elements enclosed by the same boundary (e.g. an oval outline) are perceived as belonging together.
Closure
The brain perceptually "closes the gap" on an incomplete outline, seeing a whole shape even where lines are missing.
Continuity
The eye follows the smoothest path through a crossing point, grouping curves as continuing lines rather than sharp turns.
Symmetry
Mirror-symmetric shapes or curve-pairs are perceived as belonging to the same object.
Parallelism
Lines or curves running in the same direction (e.g. wavy parallel curves) are perceived as grouped.
Key insight

None of these groupings are automatic for a computer. A human sees a flock and instantly groups it via common fate; an algorithm must be explicitly told to compute motion vectors and cluster by similarity. That gap — effortless for us, hard to formalize for algorithms — is exactly the segmentation problem this lecture builds toward (Slides 6–7).

Researched

These aren't just design heuristics borrowed for UI work — they originated as, and remain, testable psychological theories (Wertheimer 1923; Köhler and Koffka extended them). They fed directly into classical computer vision: proximity/similarity motivate clustering-based segmentation (k-means on pixel color/position), common fate motivates optical-flow-based motion segmentation, and closure/continuity motivate contour-completion and edge-linking algorithms. They also underpin why datasets like the Berkeley Segmentation Dataset exist — human-drawn segment boundaries are themselves Gestalt-grouping judgments, and different annotators disagree, showing "what defines an object" is inherently fuzzy even for people.

Wikipedia, Principles of Grouping; Scholarpedia, Gestalt principles; ScienceDirect, "The collaboration of grouping laws in vision"
Exam Alert

Why Gestalt shows up in a CV course

  • Be ready to name the specific law behind a given visual example (Kanizsa triangle = illusory contours; occluded blobs = occlusion; radial spikes = familiar configuration).
  • Gestalt grouping laws are the psychological ancestor of bottom-up segmentation algorithms: proximity/similarity to clustering, common fate to optical-flow grouping, closure/continuity to contour completion.
  • Don't dismiss these as pure "optical tricks" — examiners flag this as a common misconception; they are the conceptual justification for why bottom-up pixel-grouping segmentation works at all.
03

Top-Down vs Bottom-Up Strategies

Slides 7–8

From images to objects

A camera gives you a grid of numbers. A person looking at the same grid says "tiger on a riverbank." Segmentation is the machinery that closes that gap: take the tiger photo, and instead of a flat pixel array, produce a small number of meaningful regions — grass, water, sand, tiger — each internally consistent and distinct from its neighbors. The classic teaching example (BSDS: a tiger walking beside a blue river) shows the raw photo next to a hand-segmented version where grass becomes a green texture, water a blue band, sand a dotted region, and the tiger a hatched silhouette.

The uncomfortable question hiding underneath: what actually counts as "one object"? Is the tiger's shadow part of the tiger? Its reflection in the water? There is no single hard rule — "object" is a subjective, perceptually-defined boundary, not a physical one. That's exactly why Gestalt laws (proximity, similarity, continuation, closure, common fate) get invoked here: they're the closest thing psychology has to a formal account of how humans draw these boundaries.

Researched

The tiger image is from the Berkeley Segmentation Dataset (BSDS), built by Martin, Fowlkes, Tal & Malik at UC Berkeley (ICCV 2001). Many human volunteers segmented the same photos by hand, and the annotators disagreed substantially on where object boundaries belonged. That disagreement is the point: it's direct evidence that "ground truth" segmentation is inherently fuzzy even for humans, not a fixed target algorithms are failing to hit. BSDS became the standard benchmark for scoring algorithm boundaries against human-drawn ones for two decades.

Martin, Fowlkes, Tal & Malik, ICCV 2001 — The Berkeley Segmentation Dataset and Benchmark

Two opposite ways to attack the same problem

Once you accept segmentation is genuinely hard for a machine ("intuitive to us, hard to translate to algorithms"), there are two fundamentally different starting points:

Have a prior model of
the object (shape, class)
Top-down
→
Pixels grouped because
they belong to that object
Compare pixels by
color / texture / intensity
Bottom-up
→
Pixels grouped because

  1. Start: Image Segmentation is intuitive for humans, hard for algorithms.

    A computer has no innate notion of "object" — only numeric pixel values. The two strategies below are the two ways to bolt a notion of grouping onto that raw signal.

  2. Top-down branch.

    Assume object/semantic knowledge first — a prior model of what the target looks like (shape, class, template) — then decide which pixels are consistent with that model. Like a detective who already has a suspect and gathers evidence to fit the theory.

  3. Bottom-up branch.

    Ignore what the object "is"; just measure local similarity — color, texture, intensity — and merge pixels that look alike into growing regions. Like sorting a pile of mixed candy purely by color, with no idea what brand each piece is.

  4. Reality: most systems blend both.

    Bottom-up similarity supplies the raw grouping signal; top-down object knowledge resolves the ambiguous cases bottom-up alone gets wrong (e.g., camouflage, where object pixels genuinely resemble the background).

Exam Alert

Classic algorithms, sorted by strategy

  • Bottom-up (similarity-driven): region growing, watershed, normalized cuts (Shi & Malik, 2000), SLIC superpixels — all group pixels using local color/texture/intensity, no object model required.
  • Top-down (model-driven): Active Shape Models, Active Contours / "snakes," template-based segmentation — all require a prior expectation of object shape before fitting to the image.
  • Deep CNNs (U-Net, Mask R-CNN, DeepLab) blur the line: early filters act as learned bottom-up similarity detectors, while learned category knowledge acts as a top-down prior — both trained jointly.
  • Trap: "bottom-up is obsolete" is false — SLIC-style bottom-up superpixels are still a common fast preprocessing step inside modern deep pipelines.
  • Trap: pure bottom-up reliably fails on camouflage — object pixels statistically do resemble the background, so only top-down/object-level knowledge can still separate them.
Key insight

Top-down and bottom-up are not "old vs new" — they're two orthogonal information sources (prior knowledge vs. local similarity). Every practical segmentation method, classical or deep, is really a decision about how much weight to put on each.

04

Histograms & Thresholding

Slides 9–12

What is an image histogram?

Motivating question: "How many orange pixels are in this tiger photo?" Rather than staring at the image and guessing, sort every pixel into a bucket by its color/intensity value and count each bucket. That tally sheet is the histogram — exactly like a teacher tallying how many students scored an A, a B, a C, and so on. It answers "how many," but it throws away "where": two very different-looking images can share an identical histogram, because all spatial information is discarded.

Histogram definition
per-color count: Hf[c] = |{(x,y) : f[x,y] = c}| equivalent sum over N pixels I₁..I_N: H(v) = Σᵢ 𝟙[Iᵢ = v]

Histogram modes: the theoretical basis for thresholding

A "mode" is a hump in the histogram — an intensity value shared by many pixels. In the classic beads-on-background example, the histogram shows two humps: a tall, narrow spike for the dark bead pixels and a broader, lower hump for the lighter background pixels. "Easy to see, hard to compute" — a human glances at the plot and instantly says "two humps," but a reliable mode-counting algorithm on a noisy, jagged real-world histogram is genuinely tricky, since noise creates spurious tiny bumps.

Why this matters: a clean bimodal histogram is exactly the signal that foreground and background are separable by intensity alone. If the two humps are far apart with a clear valley between them, you can draw one dividing line (a threshold) and cleanly split the image into two classes. This is the entire theoretical justification for thresholding as a segmentation method — no thresholding algorithm can do better than the separability the histogram itself already shows.

Histogram-based / K-color segmentation

Reframe mode-finding as segmentation: the goal is to break the image into K regions by reducing the full range of colors down to K representative colors, then repainting every pixel with whichever of the K colors it's closest to. This is precisely what Photoshop's "Posterize"/"Indexed Color" does — sort a box of crayons into just K bins and re-color the drawing using only those crayons. With K=2 on the beads photo, one representative color sits inside the dark-bead peak and the other inside the light-background peak; every pixel becomes black or white depending on which side of the threshold it falls on.

Failure case: the beads speckle artifact

Run K=2 on the beads photo and the big dark spheres become clean black blobs — they sit solidly inside the dark peak, far from the threshold. But the background, though it looks visually smooth, actually spans a range of intensities, and many of its pixels sit close to the dividing line. Those borderline pixels flip unpredictably to black or white, producing a "salt speckle" dithered look across what should be a flat background. Analogy: draw one line down the middle of a room — people far from the line are unambiguous, but people milling near the line get randomly split into teams even though they're really one crowd.

Researched

This is a textbook demonstration of global thresholding, formalized by Otsu's method (1979), which picks the threshold that maximizes between-class variance for a bimodal histogram. Otsu (and global thresholding generally) works great only when the two classes' intensities are cleanly separated; it degrades wherever local variation — uneven illumination, texture — makes background pixels straddle the global cutoff. Production pipelines (ImageJ/Fiji particle analysis, industrial droplet counting) fix this speckle problem with morphological opening/closing or local/adaptive thresholding rather than a single global cutoff.

Otsu, 1979 — "A Threshold Selection Method from Gray-Level Histograms"; see also LearnOpenCV, "Otsu Thresholding with OpenCV"
Key insight

The speckle artifact is not a bug in Otsu's method — it's a direct consequence of picking colors by histogram value alone, with zero use of spatial position. Two pixels can have identical intensity while one is deep inside the object and the other is a noisy background outlier; a 1D histogram threshold can't tell them apart. This limitation is exactly what motivates moving to K-means / clustering in color space next — clustering is also intensity-driven, but it's the natural stepping stone toward incorporating additional features (position, texture) that a single global threshold cannot use.

Exam Alert

Common traps

  • A histogram counts occurrences per color/intensity value — it stores no spatial information. Two structurally different images can produce identical histograms.
  • Bimodality is a necessary condition for simple thresholding to work well, not a guarantee — Otsu specifically struggles when a histogram has more than two real peaks or very unequal class variances.
  • "K-color segmentation" here is color quantization: choosing K representative colors and mapping each pixel to its nearest one — mathematically the same objective as K-means (minimize summed squared distance to nearest center), just applied in 1D intensity space instead of full color space.
  • The speckle/salt-noise artifact under global thresholding is the standard exam example for "why do we need adaptive thresholding or clustering instead of one global cutoff."

05

K-Means Clustering

Slides 13–17

From "pick representative colors" to a clustering problem

Slide 13 poses a concrete question left over from histogram thresholding: if we want to repaint an image using only K representative colors, how do we choose them? Plot every pixel as a point in a color space (the slide uses the Red–Green plane) and the answer becomes visual — pick a handful of "center" colors ci such that every point sits as close as possible to its nearest center. That is exactly a clustering problem, and minimizing squared distance to the nearest center is K-means' entire objective.

Formally: assign every point to one of K clusters, and choose the K centers so that the total squared distance from each point to its own cluster's center is as small as possible.

K-means objective
J  =  Σi  Σp ∈ cluster i  ‖p − ci‖² — sum, over every cluster i, of the squared distance from each of its points p to that cluster's center c_i

The chicken-and-egg problem — and how it breaks in half

You can't find good centers without knowing which points belong to them, and you can't assign points without already knowing the centers. Slide 14 splits this deadlock into two questions that are each trivial on their own:

If you know the centers → assigning points is easy: for each point p, measure distance to every ci and pick the closest.
If you know the assignment → finding the best center is easy: the mean of a cluster's points minimizes total squared distance to them.

Source note

The deck labels this "break it down into subproblems" without naming the technique — it's a textbook case of alternating optimization (block coordinate descent): freeze one unknown, solve the other exactly, swap, repeat. The exact same pattern reappears one section later as EM's E-step/M-step loop.

  1. Initialize

    Randomly place K cluster centers c₁ … c_K anywhere in feature space (e.g. the R–G plane).

  2. Assign

    For every point p, compute its distance to each c_i and put p into the cluster of the closest one — a hard, all-or-nothing decision.

  3. Update

    Recompute each c_i as the mean of every point currently assigned to cluster i.

  4. Repeat

    If any c_i moved, go back to step 2 and re-assign under the new centers. Stop once nothing changes.

Convergence: guaranteed, but only locally

Every assign/update round can only decrease or hold steady the objective J — it never increases — so the loop always settles somewhere. But "somewhere" can be a local minimum: a stable arrangement that's better than any nearby alternative yet not the globally best grouping of the data. Because the very first step is a random guess, different starting centers can converge to visibly different final clusterings. The picture-strip on slide 17 makes this concrete — decision boundaries are the straight, convex edges of a Voronoi diagram, and the whole four-panel loop (init → assign → recompute → repeat) is exactly the pipeline above, run to convergence on 3 basket colors.

Researched

What the slides call "K-means" is technically Lloyd's algorithm — proposed by Stuart Lloyd at Bell Labs in 1957 (published 1982) for pulse-code modulation; the "K-means" name itself comes from James MacQueen's 1967 paper. The non-increasing-objective guarantee is real and provable, but it is a guarantee of a local minimum, never the global one.

A frequent exam misconception: K-means does not discover the "true" number of clusters — K must be chosen in advance (elbow method, silhouette score), and a poor K or an unlucky random start can visibly change the result. The standard mitigation for bad starts is K-means++, which seeds initial centers spread out (probabilistically favoring points far from existing centers) instead of picking them uniformly at random. In vision, K-means underlies color quantization, visual bag-of-words codebooks, and — as the next section shows — initializing Gaussian Mixture Models.

Lloyd's algorithm, Wikipedia · MacQueen 1967 · scikit-learn K-means docs

What K-means can't tell you

K-means always assumes roughly spherical, similarly-sized clusters, because "nearest center by Euclidean distance" carves feature space into convex, straight-edged Voronoi cells — it structurally cannot bend a boundary around an elongated or tilted cluster. It also only ever answers "which one cluster does this point belong to" with total, 100% confidence, even for a point sitting exactly on a boundary between two equally plausible clusters. Both limitations — rigid cluster shape and hard, unhedged assignment — are exactly what the probabilistic approach in the next section is built to fix.

Exam Alert

K-means traps to watch for

  • K-means is guaranteed to converge (the objective never increases) but is not guaranteed to find the global minimum — only a local one.
  • K (the number of clusters) is a required input, not something the algorithm discovers — chosen via elbow/silhouette methods or domain knowledge.
  • Sensitive to random initialization — mitigated in practice by running multiple restarts and keeping the lowest-J result, or by smarter seeding (K-means++).
  • Implicitly assumes spherical, similar-sized clusters with hard (Voronoi) boundaries — fails on elongated, overlapping, or very different-sized clusters.
  • Produces only a single hard label per point — no notion of confidence or "partial membership."

GMM & Expectation-Maximization

Slides 18–24

Two questions K-means can't answer

Slide 18 puts K-means on trial: what's the probability that point x is in cluster m? and what shape is each cluster? K-means has no answer to either — it hands back one hard label per point and implicitly treats every cluster as an equally-sized circular blob. The fix is a change of mindset: instead of "here is a pile of dots I need to sort," assume the data was generated by some underlying random process — a generative model, whose settings are collectively called θ. Once data is framed as "sampled from a distribution," precise probability questions become answerable.

Mixture of Gaussians: K fuzzy, tiltable blobs

A Mixture of Gaussians (MOG / GMM) says the dataset was produced by picking one of K Gaussian "blobs" at random — blob b chosen with probability (mixing coefficient) α_b — then dropping a point somewhere inside it, more likely near its center μ_b, with spread and tilt set by covariance matrix V_b. Unlike K-means' rigid circles, a covariance matrix can stretch and rotate a cluster into any ellipse.

Single Gaussian component density
P(x | μ_b, V_b)  =  1 / √((2π)d|V_b|)  ·  e−½(x−μ_b)ᵀ V_b−1(x−μ_b)
Overall mixture likelihood
P(x | θ)  =  Σb=1K α_b · P(x | μ_b, V_b)   subject to Σ α_b = 1

EM's goal: parameters that make the data most likely

Given the mixture form above, the natural question is: which θ = [μ1..K, V1..K, α1..K] makes the observed data as likely as possible under the model — maximizing the product of every point's individual likelihood?

Data likelihood to maximize
P(data | θ)  =  Πx P(x | θ) — product of per-point likelihoods over all data points x

Expectation-Maximization: the soft cousin of K-means' loop

We don't know in advance which blob generated which point — the same chicken-and-egg problem as slide 14, now in probabilistic form. EM breaks it exactly the way K-means did: freeze one unknown, solve the other, alternate.

  1. Initialize θ

    Pick K, then initialize μ_b, V_b, α_b for every blob — commonly seeded from a K-means run on the same data.

  2. E-step — compute ownership

    Freeze θ. For every point x_i, compute its soft "responsibility" — the probability it belongs to blob b — via Bayes' rule, given every other blob's current guess too.

  3. M-step — refit the blobs

    Freeze the responsibilities. Re-estimate each blob's mean, covariance, and mixing weight as a responsibility-weighted average over all points.

  4. Repeat

    Log-likelihood is guaranteed to never decrease each round; stop once θ (and the likelihood) stop changing.

E-step — responsibility (Bayes' rule)
P(b | x_i, μ_b, V_b)  =  α_b · P(x_i | μ_b, V_b)  /  Σk=1K α_k · P(x_i | μ_k, V_k)
M-step — weighted re-estimation
μ_bnew  =  Σi x_i · P(b|x_i)  /  Σi P(b|x_i) V_bnew  =  Σi (x_i − μ_bnew)(x_i − μ_bnew)ᵀ · P(b|x_i)  /  Σi P(b|x_i) α_bnew  =  (1/N)  Σi P(b|x_i) — new mixing weight = average responsibility blob b holds across all N points
Source note

The extracted deck recovers the E-step responsibility and covariance-update formulas directly, but the mean-update and mixing-weight images did not survive PDF extraction on this slide; they're reconstructed above in the standard GMM-EM form, consistent with the surrounding labels ("mean of blob b", "N data points", "probability blob b is selected").

Key insight

EM is literally the probabilistic generalization of the K-means loop: "assign hard label → recompute mean → repeat" becomes "assign soft responsibility → recompute weighted mean/covariance/popularity → repeat." Where K-means says a point is 100% cluster 2, EM says it might be 70% blob 1, 30% blob 2 — and that soft ownership is exactly what lets overlapping regions get sensible, non-degenerate probability under both blobs.

Why bother — applications of EM

Slide 23's list is deliberately broad, because EM is a general recipe for "observed data + a hidden variable + a probabilistic model," not just a clustering trick: any clustering problem, general model estimation, missing-data imputation, outlier detection (points with low probability under every component), color-based segmentation, motion segmentation, and foreground/background separation.

Researched

EM was formalized in the landmark 1977 Dempster–Laird–Rubin paper ("Maximum Likelihood from Incomplete Data via the EM Algorithm," JRSS), generalizing special cases that existed earlier. Real production systems lean on exactly this GMM-EM machinery: Stauffer & Grimson's 1999 per-pixel background subtraction (shipped today as OpenCV's cv2.createBackgroundSubtractorMOG2) models each pixel's color history as a mixture of Gaussians updated online; GrabCut fits a small GMM (typically 5 components) to foreground/background pixel colors and iteratively refines it with EM-like re-estimation; and GMM-HMM systems were the industry-standard acoustic model in speech recognition for decades before deep learning.

Dempster, Laird & Rubin, 1977, JRSS · Stauffer & Grimson, 1999 · OpenCV GrabCut/MOG2 docs

Where EM falls short

Local minima — each EM round never decreases the log-likelihood, but "never decreasing" only guarantees a stationary point, not the global maximum; the starting guess matters, so EM is typically run several times (or seeded from K-means) and the best result kept. Choosing K — the number of blobs must be fixed in advance, just like K-means' K; too few merges genuine groups, too many splits one true group or creates degenerate near-empty components, so K is usually chosen via BIC/AIC or domain knowledge. Model mismatch — EM assumes each cluster really is (approximately) Gaussian/elliptical; fitting a mixture of Gaussians to two interleaving crescent-shaped ("moon") clusters fails badly no matter how it's initialized, because no ellipse can represent a crescent — exactly the counterexample that motivates non-parametric alternatives like mean-shift.

Exam Alert

GMM / EM traps to watch for

  • E-step computes soft responsibilities via Bayes' rule (fixed θ); M-step re-estimates μ, V, α as responsibility-weighted statistics (fixed responsibilities) — don't swap which step does which.
  • EM guarantees monotonically non-decreasing log-likelihood, converging to a local maximum (or saddle point) — never guaranteed to be the global optimum.
  • GMM/EM is typically initialized from a K-means run — the two algorithms are complementary, not competitors.
  • K (number of components) and the choice of Gaussian as the generative shape are both assumptions you supply, not things EM discovers.
  • Mixture models exhibit label-switching: which component is called "blob 1" vs "blob 2" is arbitrary and can permute across runs — the clustering is the same, only the labeling differs.

Mean-Shift Segmentation

Slides 25–47

The core idea: climbing uphill on a density landscape

Every other clustering method in this session forces a choice up front — K-means demands a fixed K, GMM/EM demands a fixed number of Gaussian components. Mean-shift refuses to make that demand. Picture every data point as a location on a hilly terrain, where "altitude" is how densely packed nearby points are. Mean-shift drops a small window on a point, looks at everyone caught inside it, walks to their average position, and repeats — a hiker who keeps stepping toward the local crowd until there's no denser direction left to climb. Where the hiker stops is a mode (a local density peak). Every point's hike ends somewhere, and points that end at the same peak become one cluster. The number of clusters is never chosen — it falls out of however many peaks the data actually has.

Why (R, G, B, x, y) instead of just (R, G, B)

Plain color clustering (k-means on RGB, or mean-shift on RGB alone) has a blind spot: two unrelated red patches on opposite corners of an image look identical in color space and get lumped into the same cluster, even though they're not remotely the same "region." Comaniciu & Meer's fix (ICCV 1999) is to fold the pixel's spatial coordinates into the feature vector — each pixel becomes a point in a joint 5-D space: three color coordinates plus (x, y). Now two pixels only end up in the same segment if they're close in color and in the image. This joint-domain trick is also the conceptual ancestor of edge-preserving smoothing (bilateral filtering) and modern superpixel algorithms like SLIC.

One detail worth pinning down: the color coordinates used are almost always L*u*v* (CIELUV), not raw RGB. Euclidean distance in RGB doesn't track how different two colors actually look to the eye; L*u*v*/L*a*b* are designed so Euclidean distance approximates perceptual color difference, which is exactly what a distance-based mean-shift kernel needs to behave sensibly.

Kernel density estimate (KDE) — the "terrain" mean-shift climbs
f̂ₕ(x) = (1 / n·h) · Σᵢ K((x − xᵢ) / h)

Kernel density estimation, in one sentence

KDE guesses the shape of a distribution without assuming it's a neat bell curve: drop a small "bump" (kernel — usually Gaussian) on every data point, then sum all the bumps. Where points cluster, bumps overlap and stack into a tall pile — a density mode. The bandwidth h is the width of each bump: too small and every point becomes its own spike (over-segmentation); too large and distinct peaks blur into one blob (under-segmentation). This single knob does almost all the work bandwidth-tuning does across the whole KDE/mean-shift family.

Researched

The mean-shift vector was first derived by Fukunaga & Hostetler in 1975: they proved the vector from a kernel window's center to the local weighted mean of the points inside it is proportional to the gradient of the KDE surface — which is exactly why repeatedly shifting toward that mean is gradient ascent on density. The idea lay dormant until Cheng (1995) generalized it, and Comaniciu & Meer's 2002 PAMI paper ("Mean Shift: A Robust Approach Toward Feature Space Analysis") proved its convergence properties and turned it into a standard vision tool — it remains one of the most-cited papers in the field.

Fukunaga & Hostetler, IEEE Trans. Information Theory 21(1), 1975; Comaniciu & Meer, IEEE PAMI 2002
Mean-shift vector
m(x) = [ Σᵢ xᵢ·g(‖(x − xᵢ)/h‖²) ⁄ Σᵢ g(‖(x − xᵢ)/h‖²) ] − x where g = −k′ (g is the negative derivative of the kernel profile k, giving nearby points more weight than distant ones)

Reading the formula

The bracketed fraction is nothing exotic — it's the weighted centroid ("center of mass") of the points currently inside the window, with closer points weighted more heavily via g. Subtracting the window's current position x gives m(x): a displacement vector pointing from "where I am" to "where the crowd actually is." The entire algorithm is: compute this vector, move the window by it, repeat.

  1. Initialize

    Drop a window (radius = bandwidth h) on a data point or seed location in the joint feature space.

  2. Compute the center of mass

    Average the positions of all points currently captured by the window (optionally weighted by the kernel).

  3. Compute the mean-shift vector m(x)

    Subtract the window's current center from that weighted average.

  4. Translate the window by m(x)

    Re-center the window exactly on the computed centroid.

  5. Repeat until convergence

    Stop once ‖m(x)‖ falls below a small threshold ε (or a max-iteration cap) — the window has settled on a mode.

Attraction basins: how "cluster" gets defined without a K

Every starting point runs this same climb independently. The region of feature space from which all trajectories converge to the same mode is called its attraction basin — the mean-shift analogue of a watershed, where every raindrop landing inside a basin eventually flows to the same river mouth. Rather than rendering the region-of-interest/center-of-mass diagram as a static picture, it helps to read it as the following causal chain (this is the logic behind the "Slide by Y. Ukrainitz & B. Sarel" attraction-basin figure):

  1. Many starting points, one feature space

    Every data point begins its own independent mean-shift trajectory (its own "hike").

  2. Each trajectory climbs the local density gradient

    Step by step, each point's window translates toward denser neighboring territory.

  3. Trajectories converge

    Multiple, differently-started trajectories bend inward and terminate at the same peak (mode) — visually, curved paths radiating outward but collapsing to one center.

  4. Basin = the set of all points sharing that terminus

    Everything whose trajectory ends at the same mode belongs to one attraction basin.

  5. Cluster = basin

    Each basin becomes one segment/cluster — the count of segments is simply the count of basins actually discovered, never a number chosen in advance.

Key insight

Convergence is visually obvious in the animated slide sequence: the mean-shift vector arrow shrinks with each iteration and eventually disappears once the window's center exactly coincides with the center of mass of the points inside it — that resting point is the mode, and it is fully determined by the data and the bandwidth, not by any random initialization of cluster centers (unlike K-means, mean-shift has no local-minima issue in this sense).

Making it fast: speedups

Naive mean-shift is roughly O(T·n²) (T iterations, n points), because every point's neighborhood search compares against every other point — the practical bottleneck on full-resolution images. The classic fixes:

Binned estimation — coarse-grid the space and estimate density from bins instead of exact pairwise distances, cutting cost by 1–2 orders of magnitude with near-identical results. Fast neighbor search (KD-trees / ball-trees) — replace brute-force distance checks with a spatial index, the same principle scikit-learn's MeanShift uses internally. Update all windows per iteration in parallel, plus adaptive window sizing via k-NN where local density varies a lot. The reference EDISON implementation (Comaniciu & Meer's group) combines tessellation, parallel per-cell mode search, and pruning of near-duplicate mode candidates.

Researched

Modern successors push this much further: MeanShift++ (CVPR 2021) alternates grid-based mode-finding with cluster-assignment merging and reports speedups exceeding 10,000× over classic mean-shift at competitive clustering quality; a follow-up α-MeanShift++ runs another 4–7× faster while improving segmentation quality. GPU-accelerated variants apply the same philosophy for real-time microscopy cell segmentation and tracking.

Jang & Jiang, "MeanShift++: Extremely Fast Mode-Seeking," CVPR 2021 (arXiv:2104.00303)
Exam Alert

Traps this topic sets

  • Mean-shift is a mode-seeking algorithm, not a fixed-K partitioning algorithm like K-means — the number of segments emerges from however many density modes/basins exist.
  • "No K needed" does not mean "no tuning needed" — bandwidth h plays the same practical role K plays in K-means: too small over-segments, too large under-segments/merges distinct modes.
  • The mean-shift vector is gradient ascent on the estimated KDE density in feature space, not an image-intensity gradient (don't confuse it with Sobel/Canny edge gradients).
  • Convergence criterion is ‖m(x)‖ < ε (vector shrinks to ~0), not a fixed iteration count.
  • Naive mean-shift is O(n²) per iteration — that cost, not accuracy, is the main practical objection, and it's solved with KD-trees/binning, not with a different algorithm.
ProsCons
Good general-practice segmentation; solid out-of-the-box performanceKernel size (bandwidth h) must be chosen in advance
Flexible in the number and shape of regions — no assumption of blob-shaped clustersNot suitable for high-dimensional features (curse of dimensionality breaks density estimation)
Robust to outliers — driven by where data is dense, not by an average outliers can skewNaively O(n²) per iteration (mitigated by KD-trees / binning / MeanShift++)

When to reach for it

Deliberate oversegmentation as a preprocessing step before graph-based merging; generating multiple candidate segmentations; and tracking, clustering, and edge-preserving filtering applications — most visibly as OpenCV's cv2.meanShift / cv2.CamShift for object tracking. Compared to K-means it's more flexible but slower per step and more sensitive to its one hyperparameter; compared to GMM/EM, both are density-based, but GMM assumes a parametric (Gaussian) shape while mean-shift is fully nonparametric, at higher computational cost.

Q6HardThresholding
Two structurally very different images can share an identical intensity histogram. What property does the histogram formula H_f[c] = |{(x,y) : f[x,y]=c}| discard by construction?

  • CTotal pixel count

Q7EasyK-Means

  • AUpdate centers to cluster means, then assign points to the nearest center

Q8MediumK-Means
