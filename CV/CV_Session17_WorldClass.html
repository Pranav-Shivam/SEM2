Edge Devices
Deploying CV Inference at the Edge

From server racks to sensor nodes — why real CV systems increasingly run inference on the device that captured the pixels, which board fits which job, and how to answer a system-design question without reaching for a model that's too heavy to fly.

How to read this guide

Session 17 is the "where does the model actually run" session — it assumes you already know detection architectures (R-CNN family, YOLO, from Session 13) and evaluation metrics (IoU, mAP, from Sessions 13-14), and asks a new question: given a fixed board, a fixed power budget, and a fixed latency deadline, what model and hardware combination actually satisfies the constraint? Exams on this topic reward arithmetic and hardware-matching fluency — knowing the depthwise-separable convolution formula cold, knowing which chip needs INT8, and being able to write a constraint→hardware→model→metrics answer in under a minute.

01

Core Motivation — Why Not Just Call the Cloud?

Foundations

Intuition first — a camera that has to think for itself

The default architecture for a "smart camera" application used to be: capture a frame, upload it to a server, run a large model on a GPU cluster, ship the result back down. That pipeline is fine when a human is going to review the output later. It breaks the moment the system has to act on what it sees, or the moment the network itself becomes unreliable, expensive, or forbidden. Four largely independent constraints push inference physically onto the device holding the camera: latency, bandwidth, privacy / offline operation, and power / thermals. Every edge-deployment exam question is really asking you to identify which of these four is binding in a given scenario.

Latency — round-trip time you can't get back

A cloud pipeline's total latency is capture + upload + network round-trip + server inference + download. Even if the server-side GPU is far faster per-frame than any edge board, the network round-trip usually dominates and — worse — is unreliable: a dropped packet, a congested cell tower, or a tunnel with no signal turns "slow" into "no detection at all." An edge pipeline collapses this to just capture + local inference, with no network term at all.

Real-time pedestrian detection in an autonomous vehicle is the canonical example: the car is moving the entire time the detector is "thinking," so every millisecond of pipeline latency converts directly into distance traveled blind — exactly the distance = speed × time arithmetic from the detector speed/accuracy comparisons in Session 13, except now the "time" term includes a network round trip that the vehicle has no control over.

Bandwidth — sending an answer, not a video

Streaming raw or lightly compressed video is enormously more expensive than streaming what a detector actually needs to report. A worked, order-of-magnitude comparison for a 720p, 30 fps stream:

Bandwidth, worked example — raw pixels vs. compressed video vs. detections-only
Raw uncompressed: 1280×720×3 bytes/frame × 30 fps = 82,944,000 B/s ≈ 663 Mbps H.264/H.265 compressed 720p30 (typical): roughly 2–5 Mbps — still a real, continuous uplink cost Detections only (≤20 boxes/frame, 6 numbers/box, 4 B/number): 20 × 6 × 4 = 480 B/frame × 30 fps = 14,400 B/s ≈ 0.115 Mbps → Sending boxes instead of compressed video is ~20–40× lighter; instead of raw pixels, ~5000× lighter.
Reading this the right way

The exact multiplier isn't the point to memorize — the pattern is: whatever you transmit scales with the size of the representation, not the size of the scene. A handful of floating-point numbers describing "person at (x,y), 0.94 confidence" costs almost nothing to move; the pixels that produced that answer cost a great deal. Running the model at the edge means you only ever have to move the cheap representation.

Privacy / offline operation — some pipelines can't phone home

A factory floor doing defect inspection, a badge-free attendance system reading faces, or a drone that has flown out of radio range all share the same requirement: the vision pipeline must produce a usable answer with zero dependency on a network connection, either because none exists, because latency-sensitive control loops can't wait for one, or because raw video of people or proprietary equipment must never leave the premises. Edge inference isn't just faster in these cases — it's the only architecture that satisfies the constraint at all.

Power / thermals — the budget that picks the hardware

A battery-powered drone or a fanless enclosure on a factory wall has a hard power ceiling; exceed it and the device either dies early or throttles its own clock speed to avoid overheating, silently degrading the very latency you were trying to guarantee. In practice, the power budget is often the first constraint that eliminates candidate hardware in a system-design question — a GPU workstation is disqualified before you even get to compare its accuracy.

Why This Matters — the exam always asks "why edge, not cloud"

Whenever a question describes a scenario, scan it for these four signatures: a moving system or safety loop → latency; a described video/camera feed with a mention of connectivity cost → bandwidth; a location with no reliable network, or a mention of sensitive footage → privacy/offline; a battery, drone, or "fanless"/"low-power" descriptor → power/thermals. Most real scenarios combine two or three of these at once — identify all of them before choosing hardware in Section 05.

02

Hardware Classification Matrix

Foundations

Intuition first — a ladder, not a single "edge device"

"Edge device" is not one thing. It spans a ladder from a $5 microcontroller that can barely hold a few hundred kilobytes of model weights, up to a purpose-built neural accelerator chip. Climbing the ladder buys you more raw compute and more framework flexibility, at the cost of more power draw, more money, and (for the microcontroller end) an operating system you don't even get to have. The exam expects you to recognize keywords — "ESP32," "OpenVINO," "TensorRT," "Edge TPU" — and instantly place them on this ladder.

ClassExamplesTypical Application
MCU + cameraESP32-CAMTiny models, motion trigger, JPEG snapshot. Not YOLO-v8
Single-Board Computer (ARM)Raspberry Pi, Banana PiOpenCV + small CNN (MobileNet, YOLO-nano) via Python/C++; USB camera support
Intel CPU / NUCCore, Atom, NUCOpenVINO + OneAPI; Movidius VPUs as USB accelerator
NVIDIA JetsonNano, TX2, OrinCUDA/cuDNN, TensorRT, DeepStream for multi-camera pipelines
Google CoralEdge TPUINT8 TensorFlow Lite models only; highest inferences-per-second per watt with TPU compilation

What each rung actually buys you

MCU + camera (ESP32-CAM): no real operating system, kilobytes to low megabytes of RAM, and models measured in hundreds of kilobytes. Good for "is something moving" triggers or capturing a JPEG snapshot to hand off elsewhere — structurally incapable of running a modern detector like YOLOv8.

Single-Board Computer / ARM (Raspberry Pi, Banana Pi): a full Linux userspace and a general-purpose ARM CPU. This is enough to run OpenCV plus a genuinely small CNN — MobileNet-class classifiers, YOLO-nano-class detectors — through ordinary Python or C++, with normal USB camera support. What it does not have is dedicated matrix-multiply silicon, so CPU-only inference on anything larger tends to blow through real-time latency budgets.

Intel CPU / NUC (Core, Atom): paired with Intel's OpenVINO toolkit and OneAPI, these compile a trained network into a hardware-optimized inference graph. A Movidius Vision Processing Unit (VPU), plugged in as a USB accelerator (the Neural Compute Stick line), adds dedicated low-power inference silicon without needing a discrete GPU.

NVIDIA Jetson (Nano, TX2, Orin): the most capable and most flexible class — real CUDA cores and, on newer boards, Tensor Cores, programmable with the same CUDA/cuDNN stack used on data-center GPUs. TensorRT compiles and optimizes a network for the specific Jetson chip; DeepStream is NVIDIA's SDK for building multi-camera pipelines (decode, batch, infer, track) that would otherwise require a lot of hand-written glue code.

Google Coral (Edge TPU): a purpose-built ASIC that only executes INT8-quantized TensorFlow Lite graphs, but does so with the best inferences-per-second-per-watt of any class here — the trade is rigidity (fully quantize to INT8 and stay within Edge-TPU-supported ops, or the unsupported layers silently fall back to the host CPU and become the new bottleneck).

03

Efficient Model Architectures — Depthwise-Separable Convolutions

Foundations

Intuition first — split "where" from "which channels"

A standard convolution filter does two jobs simultaneously at every output pixel: it looks at a spatial neighborhood (e.g. 3×3) and it mixes information across every input channel to produce every output channel. MobileNet and Xception both ask: what if we split those two jobs into two separate, much cheaper operations? First a depthwise convolution applies one small spatial filter per channel independently (no cross-channel mixing at all), then a pointwise 1×1 convolution mixes channels together with no spatial extent. Chaining these two cheap steps approximates what the single expensive standard convolution did — this is exactly the trick that makes MobileNet-class networks small enough to run on a Raspberry Pi or Coral.

The depthwise-separable convolution formula
Standard 3×3 convolution across C channels: 9·C² multiplications per pixel Depthwise-separable decomposition: Depthwise 3×3: 9·C multiplications (one 3×3 filter per channel, no mixing) Pointwise 1×1: C² multiplications (mixes channels, no spatial extent) Total: 9·C + C²

Worked example — C = 256 channels

  1. Standard: 9 × 256² = 9 × 65,536 = 589,824 multiplications per pixel.

  2. Depthwise: 9 × 256 = 2,304 multiplications per pixel.

  3. Pointwise: 256² = 65,536 multiplications per pixel.

  4. Depthwise-separable total: 2,304 + 65,536 = 67,840 multiplications per pixel.

  5. Speedup ratio: 589,824 / 67,840 ≈ 8.69×.

Answer≈8.7× fewer multiplications at C=256 — matching the "roughly 8-9× computational efficiency" figure quoted for depthwise-separable convolutions versus a standard convolution.

Detection-specific compression and quantization

YOLO-nano and YOLO-tiny apply the same "shrink the backbone" philosophy specifically to detection architectures — fewer layers, fewer channels, sometimes a smaller input resolution — trading some mAP for a large drop in latency and memory footprint, the same trade YOLO itself made against the R-CNN family in Session 13. On top of architectural compression, quantization to INT8 — representing weights and activations with 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floats — is critical for two specific hardware targets named in Section 02: it's mandatory for Coral (Edge TPU accepts INT8 TFLite only) and strongly recommended for OpenVINO deployments, where INT8 execution roughly quarters the memory footprint and lets fixed-point ALUs run at much higher throughput than floating-point units of the same silicon area.

Research Enhanced

MobileNet's own paper generalizes the C=256 arithmetic above into a single reduction factor: for an N-channel output and a Dk×Dk kernel, depthwise-separable computation is 1/N + 1/Dk² times the standard convolution's cost. For the common Dk=3 case, as N grows large the 1/N term vanishes and the ratio approaches 1/9 — i.e. roughly a 9× reduction, consistent with the worked example above. Architectures on the opposite end of the spectrum — Meta's Segment Anything Model (SAM) and large Vision Transformers — remain squarely cloud-based; their attention mechanisms scale with sequence length in a way no current edge accelerator budget can absorb. The one crossover point worth knowing: a genuinely small variant, ViT-Tiny, is compact enough to execute on a Jetson Orin's TOPS budget, even though its full-size ViT siblings are not.

Howard et al., "MobileNets: Efficient Convolutional Neural Networks for Mobile Vision Applications," 2017 · Kirillov et al., "Segment Anything," ICCV 2023 · Dosovitskiy et al., "An Image is Worth 16x16 Words" (ViT), ICLR 2021

Exam Radar

Depthwise-separable convolutions — exam radar

  • Memorize the three terms exactly: standard = 9C², depthwise = 9C, pointwise = C² — a numeric question will hand you a channel count and expect all three plus the ratio.
  • Common mistake: forgetting to add the depthwise and pointwise terms together before comparing to the standard convolution — comparing standard against depthwise alone hugely overstates the speedup.
  • Know why the ratio approaches ~9× (not some other number) for large C: it's driven purely by the 9 in "9·C²" versus the dominant "C²" pointwise term, tying straight back to the 3×3 kernel size (Dk²=9).
04

Performance Metrics to Report

Foundations

Intuition first — one number is never the answer

Reporting only accuracy, or only FLOPs, or only frames-per-second, always hides half of the deployment story. A complete evaluation of an edge CV system reports five distinct kinds of measurement, each answering a different question a reviewer or exam grader will ask.

MetricPurpose
Accuracy / mAP / mIoUDetermines task suitability
FLOPsTheoretical compute per image
Memory footprint (FP32/FP16/INT8)Verifies device RAM compatibility
Inference time on specified hardwareThe only meaningful latency measurement — always pair model with board
Power (W) / inferences-per-second per WattBattery life and thermal constraints
Why This Matters — FLOPs alone is a trap

FLOPs is a theoretical count of multiply-accumulate operations — it says nothing about memory-bandwidth bottlenecks, kernel implementation quality, framework overhead, or thermal throttling, all of which shift real wall-clock latency independently of the FLOPs number. Two models with identical FLOPs can post very different latencies on the same board; the same model can post very different latencies on two different boards. This is precisely why "inference time" in the table above is qualified as "on specified hardware" — a latency number without a named board attached is not a valid answer to a deployment question.

A subtle version of this trap: depthwise-separable convolutions cut FLOPs by ~8-9× (Section 03), but on some accelerators the depthwise step is memory-bandwidth-bound rather than compute-bound, so the real measured speedup on that specific chip can come in noticeably below the FLOPs-based prediction. FLOPs bounds the theoretical best case; only a measurement on the actual board tells you the truth.

Inferences-per-second per Watt — worked example
Inferences-per-second per Watt = throughput (inferences/s) ÷ power draw (W) Example: a Coral Edge TPU model running at 400 inferences/s while drawing 2W: 400 ÷ 2 = 200 inferences/s per Watt
Exam Radar

Metrics — exam radar

  • If a question gives you FLOPs and asks "is this fast enough," the honest answer is "cannot be determined without a measured latency on the target board."
  • Memory footprint has to be checked per precision (FP32/FP16/INT8) — a model that fits in RAM at INT8 may not fit at FP32, and boards like the ESP32-CAM or a Raspberry Pi have hard RAM ceilings that FLOPs numbers won't reveal.
  • Always report power alongside speed — a detector that hits 100 FPS by drawing 60W is not a valid answer for a battery-powered drone, no matter how good its FPS number looks in isolation.
05

Exam-Style System Design Walkthrough

Foundations

Scenario

Warehouse bay occupancy detection; <100ms latency, no cloud connectivity, ~8W power envelope.

Solution architecture, piece by piece

Sensor: a fixed IP camera or Pi camera; 640×480 resolution is adequate for person detection at typical warehouse-bay standoff distances — no need to pay the compute cost of a higher resolution the detector doesn't need.

Processing board: Coral or Jetson Nano. A Raspberry Pi 4 running CPU-only inference is insufficient for the 100ms budget — general-purpose ARM cores lack the parallel matrix-multiply throughput that a dedicated accelerator (Edge TPU) or a small GPU (Jetson Nano) provides, so the same detector that comfortably clears 100ms on Coral or Jetson can miss it by a wide margin on Pi-CPU alone.

Model: INT8 YOLO-tiny or SSD-MobileNet — not Faster R-CNN (multi-stage, too slow for the deadline) and not SAM (a segmentation foundation model with far more compute than the task needs, and not detection-shaped output to begin with).

Pipeline: capture → letterbox normalization → inference → NMS → geometric intersection check (person ∩ bay polygon) → GPIO alarm trigger; no video uplink at any stage — nothing about the pipeline requires transmitting a frame anywhere.

Reported metrics: mAP @ IoU 0.5 on a domain-specific validation set (real warehouse-bay footage, not a generic benchmark); latency measured on the actual target board; wall-power consumption under load.

  1. Capture

    Grab a frame from the fixed camera at the bay.

  2. Letterbox normalization

    Resize/pad the frame to the detector's fixed input shape without distorting aspect ratio.

  3. Inference

    Run the quantized INT8 detector on-device.

  4. Non-Max Suppression

    Collapse duplicate overlapping boxes per class (Session 13's NMS, unchanged here).

  5. Geometric intersection check

    Test whether a detected person's box intersects the bay's defined polygon region — pure geometry, no further model inference needed.

  6. GPIO alarm trigger

    Flip a physical output pin to sound/light an alarm — the entire loop closes on-device, with no video ever leaving the board.

The answer template — memorize this order

  1. Constraint identification — pull out every latency, bandwidth, privacy, and power constraint the question states or implies.

  2. Hardware class selection — use the constraints (especially the power budget and the latency deadline) to pick a rung on the Section 02 ladder.

  3. Model family choice — pick an architecture whose accuracy/speed profile fits the deadline, favoring MobileNet-style backbones or YOLO-tiny/nano variants for tight budgets.

  4. Quantization strategy — state whether INT8 is mandatory (Coral) or merely beneficial (OpenVINO/Jetson), and note the memory-footprint payoff.

  5. Device-side post-processing — describe what happens after the raw model output (NMS, geometry checks, alarms) entirely on-device, with no uplink.

  6. Three quantitative results — always close with exactly three numbers: an accuracy metric (mAP/IoU), a measured latency in milliseconds on the named board, and a power draw in watts.

Exam Radar

System design — exam radar

  • Reaching for Faster R-CNN or SAM in a <100ms edge answer is an instant red flag to a grader — name a lightweight, quantization-friendly model instead.
  • Forgetting to name a specific board (just saying "an edge device") loses credit — "Coral" or "Jetson Nano," not "some accelerator."
  • The answer isn't complete without the closing three numbers (mAP, ms, W) — a design description alone, with no quantitative claims, reads as incomplete.
06

Conceptual Deep Dives

Compare & Contrast
Why does a Raspberry Pi 4 (CPU-only) struggle to hit a <100ms detection deadline that a Jetson Nano clears comfortably, even running similar-sized models?

  1. A CNN's core operation — convolution — is dominated by large numbers of independent multiply-accumulate operations that can, in principle, run in parallel across many small cores simultaneously.

  2. A general-purpose ARM CPU (Pi 4) has a handful of cores optimized for sequential, branching, general-purpose code — it executes those multiply-accumulates mostly serially (with limited SIMD help), so wall-clock time scales close to linearly with FLOPs.

  3. A Jetson Nano's CUDA cores are purpose-built for exactly this kind of massively parallel arithmetic — the same FLOPs count is spread across hundreds of cores at once, so measured latency comes in far below what the Pi's serial execution model achieves for an identical network.

Key Insight

Two boards can have "similar" advertised compute numbers and still differ by 5-10× in measured detector latency — parallelism, not raw FLOPs capacity, is what closes a tight latency budget.

If Model A has fewer FLOPs than Model B, is Model A guaranteed to run faster on a given edge board?

  1. Not necessarily. FLOPs counts arithmetic operations only — it says nothing about how much data has to move between memory and compute units, which is often the real bottleneck on small edge chips with limited memory bandwidth.

  2. Depthwise convolutions are a concrete example: they're extremely FLOP-light (Section 03), but because each channel is processed with very little arithmetic per byte loaded, they can be memory-bandwidth-bound on some hardware, sometimes running slower in wall-clock terms than their tiny FLOPs count would suggest.

  3. This is exactly why Section 04's metrics table insists on "inference time on specified hardware" as its own separate, mandatory row — FLOPs is a useful upper-bound estimate during design, but only a measurement on the real board is trustworthy for a latency claim.

Key Insight

FLOPs predicts theoretical compute cost; only measured latency on the named board predicts what a deployed system will actually do.

Why does quantizing a CV model's weights and activations from FP32 to INT8 usually preserve most of its accuracy, and why do edge accelerators specifically demand it?

  1. CNN weights and activations are typically distributed over a narrow, well-behaved numeric range after training — mapping that range onto 256 integer levels (INT8) loses precision, but the loss is usually small relative to the model's existing tolerance for noise (the same tolerance that lets dropout and data augmentation work at all).

  2. Edge accelerators (Coral's Edge TPU, and Movidius VPUs under OpenVINO) build their arithmetic units around fixed-point INT8 multiply-accumulate circuits rather than floating-point ones, because INT8 ALUs are smaller, cheaper, and far more power-efficient per operation — the entire "inferences-per-second per Watt" advantage from Section 02 depends on this design choice.

  3. The trade-off surfaces at the edges of the distribution: a model with a few outlier activation values, or one that wasn't quantization-aware trained, can see a real accuracy drop — which is why "quantize to INT8" is a strategy to state explicitly in a system-design answer, not an assumption to leave silent.

Key Insight

INT8 isn't just a memory-saving trick — it's the numeric format the fastest, most power-efficient edge silicon is physically built around.

The template closes with exactly three numbers: an accuracy metric (mAP/mIoU/accuracy), a measured latency in milliseconds on the specific target board, and a power draw in watts — the same three dimensions the Performance Metrics table (Section 04) insists must always be reported together.
