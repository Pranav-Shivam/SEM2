01 Where this lecture starts

Logic is brittle. The world isn't binary.

Rules either fire or they don't — there's no way to say "usually true, but not always." Probability is the new foundation that lets an agent hold a belief that can bend without breaking.

Monotonic vs. non-monotonic reasoning

Classical logic only ever adds to what you know — new facts never retract old conclusions. Real agents need the opposite: the ability to believe "birds fly," then retract that belief the moment they learn "this bird is a penguin."

MonotonicNon-Monotonic
ConsistentRelaxed consistency
Complete knowledgeIncomplete knowledge
StaticDynamic
DiscreteContinuous & learning agent
Predicate logicProbabilistic model
// field note

Non-monotonic logics (default logic, circumscription) were built in AI in the late 1970s–80s specifically for the "penguin problem" — classical logic can't gracefully represent exceptions. Probability sidesteps the debate entirely: "flies" just gets a high probability for birds generally, and a low one once "penguin" is observed — updated smoothly, never retracted as a broken rule. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea_Pearl)

Why an agent needs uncertainty at all

Sensors are noisy, actuators don't always do what they're told, and information is rarely complete. Logic can't encode exceptions or statistical properties — probability can, in a single number.

Partial observability

Chance nodes, sensor noise, actuator error — the agent never sees the whole board.

Brittle logic

Can't encode "usually true" or capture statistical regularities in a domain.

Data-driven era

Massive data + statistics let the data do the work — the basis for most modern learning.

02 Self-study refresher

Sample spaces, axioms, conditioning, and the chain rule

Four building blocks, all leading to one destination: the chain rule, which becomes the mathematical backbone of every Bayesian network in this deck.

Sample space & events

The sample space S is every possible outcome (6 faces of a die). Each outcome is a sample point. An event is any subset you care about, e.g. "roll < 4". A random variable just relabels outcomes — e.g. Weather maps each world to sunny/rain/cloudy/snow.

Types of random variables

Boolean — two values (Cavity: yes/no). Discrete — a fixed list of exhaustive, mutually exclusive values (Weather). Continuous — any value in a range (Temp = 21.6, or Temp < 22.0).

Kolmogorov's axioms

0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1 P(true) = 1 P(false) = 0 P(A ∨ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∧ B) ← subtract the double-counted overlap

Conditional probability

"Given that B is all I know, how likely is A?" — zoom into the world of B, then ask what fraction of it is also A.

P(A | B) = P(A ∧ B) / P(B) P(cavity | toothache) = 0.8 ← knowing only "toothache," 80% chance of cavity P(cavity | toothache, cavity) = 1 ← already told the answer P(cavity | toothache, sunny) = P(cavity | toothache) ← irrelevant evidence changes nothing

The chain rule (product rule)

Breaks one big, scary joint probability into a chain of small conditional steps — this single identity is what a Bayesian network is built to shortcut.

P(X₁,…,Xₙ) = P(Xₙ|X₁..Xₙ−₁) · P(Xₙ−₁|X₁..Xₙ−₂) · … · P(X₁) = ∏ᵢ P(Xᵢ | X₁,…,Xᵢ−₁)

Independence

Two events are independent when knowing one tells you nothing new about the other.

A ⊥ B ⇔ P(A|B) = P(A) ⇔ P(A ∧ B) = P(A)·P(B)

The complexity problem

This 3-variable table only needed 8 numbers. Real problems don't stay that small.

Time = O(dⁿ) Space = O(dⁿ) d = max arity, n = # variables 30 Boolean variables → 2³⁰ > 1 billion numbers — prohibitive
// field note — the curse, formalized

This exponential blow-up is a concrete "curse of dimensionality." It's also why, even after Bayesian networks compress the representation, exact inference on them remains NP-hard in the worst case — a result proven by Gregory Cooper in 1990 — which is the direct justification for the Approximate Inference module later in this app. (sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/000437029090060D)

04 Flip the conditional, then compress the table

Bayes' Rule gets you the hard direction. Networks get you the small table.

One formula lets you compute P(cause | effect) from the much-easier-to-measure P(effect | cause). One data structure turns an exponential table into a handful of small local ones.

Bayes' Rule

P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B)
// field note — Thomas Bayes, and where this shows up today

Named for Rev. Thomas Bayes (1701–1761); his work was published posthumously in 1763. Modern uses are everywhere: Naive Bayes spam filters (Gmail's original filter was Bayesian at its core) compute P(spam | words) from the easier P(words | spam); medical diagnosis turns P(symptom | disease) into P(disease | symptom) — and combined with a low base-rate prior, this is exactly why rare-disease screening tests generate so many false positives. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Bayes)

Bayesian Network — the syntax

A graphical notation for conditional independence, and hence for compactly specifying a full joint distribution.

Nodes

One node per random variable.

Edges

A directed, acyclic graph (DAG) — an arrow means "directly influences."

CPT

Each node stores P(Xᵢ | Parents(Xᵢ)) — a small local table, not the whole joint.

// field note — Judea Pearl, 1985

Judea Pearl introduced Bayesian networks in 1985, coining the term to emphasize the subjective nature of inputs, reliance on Bayesian conditioning, and the causal/evidential distinction. His work is credited with moving AI away from brittle 1970s–80s rule-based expert systems toward principled probabilistic reasoning — work that won him the 2011 ACM Turing Award. (amturing.acm.org/award_winners/pearl_2658896.cfm)

05 Two textbook networks

Toothache/Catch, and the most famous alarm in AI

The first network shows plain conditional independence. The second — Burglary/Earthquake/Alarm/John/Mary — is the single most reused teaching example in Bayesian-network history.

Network #1 — Weather, Cavity, Toothache, Catch

Cavity is the common cause of both Toothache and Catch. Once Cavity is known, Toothache and Catch tell you nothing more about each other — conditionally independent given Cavity. Weather sits off on its own, unconnected.

Exercise — P(good weather, ¬toothache, ¬cavity, catch)

The network structure collapses the chain rule down to just 4 small terms — no 16-row joint table needed:

P(Weather, ¬Toothache, ¬Cavity, Catch) = P(Weather) · P(¬Cavity) · P(¬Toothache | ¬Cavity) · P(Catch | ¬Cavity) ← Weather's own prior × Cavity's own prior × Toothache/Catch each conditioned only on Cavity

Network #2 — The Burglary Alarm

Two independent root causes (Burglary, Earthquake) can trigger the Alarm; the Alarm can cause two neighbors to call.

Worked joint probability

"Alarm sounded, but neither burglary nor earthquake happened, and both John and Mary called":

P(j,m,a,¬b,¬e) = P(j|a)·P(m|a)·P(a|¬b,¬e)·P(¬b)·P(¬e) = 0.90 × 0.70 × 0.001 × 0.999 × 0.998 ≈ 0.000628
// field note — the most famous alarm in AI

This exact network (same CPT values) traces to Judea Pearl's original work and was popularized worldwide via Russell & Norvig's AIMA, where it remains the flagship example for exact inference. It's small enough to compute by hand, yet demonstrates causal reasoning, diagnostic reasoning, and "explaining away" all at once — see Module 7. (people.cs.pitt.edu/~milos/courses/cs2740/Lectures/class19.pdf)

06 Building a network from a word problem

Traffic to the airport — growing a graph one cause at a time

A home-grown example that shows the actual skill of Bayesian-network design: read a paragraph, find the variables, and decide which way the arrows point.

The scenario

A festival or political rally causes a procession; a procession (plus weekend) causes a road block; a road block (plus weekend traffic) reroutes the car; rerouting makes the traveler late. Weather@BLR sits off to the side — it affects accommodation plans, not travel time, so it's not wired into the lateness chain at all.

Two general independence rules (any Bayesian network)

Rule 1

A node is conditionally independent of its non-descendants, given its parents.

Rule 2 — Markov Blanket

A node is conditionally independent of every other node, given its parents, children, and children's other parents.

// field note — Markov blankets in practice

The Markov blanket defines the minimal set of nodes that fully insulates a variable from the rest of the network. It's used heavily for efficient inference and structure learning — some ML feature-selection algorithms use it directly to find the minimal "relevant" variable set for predicting a target, discarding everything else without losing predictive power. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_network#Markov_blanket)

Exercise — classify these traffic-network queries

A joint query pins every variable to a value at once; a conditional query asks for one variable given another is already known — computed as a ratio, P(A|B) = P(A∧B)/P(B).

QueryType
P(Late, Weekend, ¬RoadBlock, ¬Festival, ¬CarsRouted)Joint
P(Festival | CarsRouted)Conditional
P(Late | Festival)Conditional
07 Same graph, four directions

Diagnostic, causal, inter-causal, mixed

The same network answers questions running with the arrows, against them, sideways through a shared effect, or all three tangled together.

Diagnostic

Effect → cause. "The alarm rang — was there a burglary?" Reasoning against the arrows.

Causal

Cause → effect. "There's a burglary — will the alarm ring?" Reasoning with the arrows.

Inter-causal

Two causes, one shared effect. Learning about one changes belief in the other — "explaining away."

Mixed

Real questions often combine all three at once, using scattered evidence.

Exercise — classify these three queries

Using the Burglary/Earthquake/Alarm/JohnCalls/MaryCalls network:

QueryReasoningType
P(J | B, ¬A)Evidence (B, A) sits above the query J in the chain — reasoning with the arrowsCausal
P(E | B, M)M is a descendant (diagnostic), B is a co-parent sharing child Alarm (inter-causal)Mixed
P(A | J)Reasoning from effect J back up to its direct cause ADiagnostic
// field note — explaining away

Two causes of a shared effect are marginally independent, but become dependent once you condition on the shared effect. Learning the alarm sounded raises suspicion of both burglary and earthquake; additionally learning an earthquake occurred then lowers the burglary probability back down — even though burglary and earthquake have no direct link. This is the signature phenomenon that makes Bayesian networks more powerful than naive rule chaining.

08 Marginalize the hidden variables

P(Burglary | John & Mary called) = 28.47%

The full hand-cranked calculation: sum out the hidden variables (Alarm, Earthquake), then normalize.

The normalization trick

Since P(B|JM) + P(¬B|JM) = 1, you never need P(JM) directly — compute both unnormalized joint terms, then divide each by their sum.

P(B | J,M) = P(B,J,M) / [ P(B,J,M) + P(¬B,J,M) ] ← α = normalizing constant

Marginalizing out Alarm and Earthquake

A and E are hidden (neither query nor evidence) — sum over every combination of their values, applying the chain rule at each step:

P(B,J,M) = ∑ᵩ ∑ᵞ P(J|A)·P(M|A)·P(A|B,E)·P(B)·P(E)

P(B,J,M)

0.000001197 + 0.00000000005 + 0.000592 + 0.0000002994
≈ 0.0005935

P(¬B,J,M)

0.0003650346 + 0.00000070929 + 0.00062811126 + 0.000498002499
≈ 0.001492

P(B | J,M) = 0.0005935 / (0.0005935 + 0.001492) = 0.2847
// field note — sanity check the result

28.47% is far above the raw prior P(Burglary)=0.001 (0.1%) — two phone calls are strong evidence — yet still under 50/50, because false alarms and false-positive calls remain plausible alternative explanations. Exercise: apply the same technique to compute P(JohnCalls | Earthquake), marginalizing out Burglary and Alarm instead.

09 Skip what you can prove is irrelevant

Read independence off the graph — before doing any arithmetic

D-separation is a purely visual test. Variable Elimination uses it to decide which variables never need to be touched at all.

D-separation

Two variables are d-separated if they are conditionally independent given some evidence — readable straight off the DAG, no arithmetic required.

XYEvidence Zd-separated?
FestivalWeatherCars routedNo
LateWeatherRoad blockNo
Road blockLateCars routedYes
All bars fullRoad blockCars routedNo
P(RoadBlock | Late, CarsRouted) = P(RoadBlock | Late) → once Cars-routed is known, Road block adds nothing more about Lateness
// field note — the formal rule

D-separation checks every path between two nodes: chains/forks are blocked if the middle node is in the evidence; colliders (v-structures) are blocked unless the middle node or a descendant is in the evidence — the opposite rule, and the reason "explaining away" works. It's proven sound and complete: whatever it says about independence is guaranteed true in every distribution the graph can represent. (helenedk.medium.com/bayesian-networks-d-separation)

Variable elimination — drop the irrelevant nodes

"All bars are full" only actually depends on weekend — nothing else. Even in a 7-variable network, the query collapses instantly:

P(AllBarsFull) = ∑⅂∑₁∑𝑋∑₂∑ℱ P(L,C,B,W,R,F) = P(B | W) ← L, C, R, F are neither query, evidence, nor ancestors of (W,B) — eliminate them

Full worked elimination — the Fever network

Viral→fLu→Fever←foodPoison. Eliminate one variable at a time, keeping only small local factor tables.

Step 1 — eliminate V

Multiply P(V) × P(L|V), sum out V → P(L=T) = 0.48+0.004 = 0.484, P(L=F) = 0.516

Step 2 — eliminate L, D

Combine P(L), P(D), P(F|L,D) into an 8-row factor, sum out L and D → P(Fever=T) = 0.461, P(Fever=F) = 0.539

// field note — why this beats plain enumeration

At no point does Variable Elimination build a table over all four variables at once — only 2- and 3-variable intermediate factors, one elimination at a time. Formalized by Zhang & Poole (1994); worst-case complexity O(dr) where r is the largest intermediate factor size, and finding the optimal elimination order is itself NP-hard — so real systems use heuristics (min-degree, min-fill). (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_elimination)

10 When exact is too slow

Prior Sampling → Rejection Sampling → Likelihood Weighting

Three sampling methods, each fixing the previous one's weakness: from "count everything" to "don't waste a single sample."

1 · Prior Sampling

Roll a random number against each node's CPT, in topological order (parents before children). Repeat thousands of times, then answer any query by counting matching sample rows — e.g. P(L)=3/8, P(L|F)=3/5.

2 · Rejection Sampling

Same generation process, but discard any sample that doesn't match the evidence before counting. Fixes nothing about wasted samples — if evidence is rare, most generated samples get thrown away. P(F|D) = 5/8 in the worked example.

3 · Likelihood Weighting

Fix evidence variables directly (never sample them), sample only the rest, and give every sample a weight = product of the evidence CPT probabilities. No sample is ever wasted.

Likelihood weighting, worked

P(F | D,¬V) = (0.04 + 0.04) / (4 × 0.04) ← V,D are root evidence: weight = P(¬V)·P(D) = 0.4×0.1 = 0.04, same for every sample P(F | D,¬L) = (0.099 + 0.099) / (3×0.099 + 0.02) ← L depends on sampled V, so weight varies per sample (0.099 if V=F, 0.02 if V=T)
answer = ( ∑ weight of samples where query is TRUE ) / ( ∑ weight of ALL samples )
// field note — Monte Carlo, and why LW usually wins

This whole family — simulate, then estimate by counting/weighting — is Monte Carlo, first used systematically by Ulam and von Neumann at Los Alamos in the 1940s to simulate neutron diffusion. Likelihood Weighting is the most commonly implemented approximate-inference method in practice, since it never discards a sample and typically converges much faster than Rejection Sampling when evidence is rare — though it can still struggle when many evidence variables downstream of sampled ones make weights small and highly variable, which is what motivates further methods like MCMC/Gibbs sampling. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method)

Exercise B — The cricket/IPL network

"Most WILP students are cricket fans; being a parent reduces watching; watching reduces next-day concentration; viral infection (common in rainy season) is a second cause of reduced concentration; reduced concentration raises stress (hurting work productivity) and lowers exam grades."

Two variants of this word problem appear in the source deck, same network, one flipped premise: variant 1 asks about a student not affected by viral infection, variant 2 asks about one who is affected. Both pose the same three tasks: (1) construct the network, (2) compute the full joint probability for the stated combination of values, (3) check whether Grades ⊥ Stress given only Viral holds — via d-separation.

// field note — the d-separation trap in both exercises

Both exercises ask: is Grades independent of Stress, given only Viral infection is known? Trace the path Grades←Concentration→Stress — it runs through Concentration, not Viral. Conditioning on a different upstream cause of Concentration does not block that path, so Grades and Stress remain d-connected. You'd need to condition on Concentration itself to separate them — a common trap when a network has more than one root cause feeding the same intermediate node.
